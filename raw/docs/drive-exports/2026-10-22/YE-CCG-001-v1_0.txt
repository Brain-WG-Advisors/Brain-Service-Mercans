Module 1: Foundational Jurisdictional Parameters
Yemen operates a single pre-war statutory framework across both the internationally recognised Government (Aden) and the Sana'a-based de-facto authority: Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 and its Executive Regulations, Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 (as amended by Laws 25/1997, 11/2001 and 25/2003), and the Social Insurance Law of 1991. The divergence between the two zones is administrative, not statutory (two tax and social-security administrations, two central banks, two exchange rates). All statutory amounts in this guide are therefore stated in Yemeni Rial (YER); a USD benchmark is used only where unavoidable, and then with the applicable market rate named.
1.1 Fiscal, Currency, and Language
What is the statutory tax year?
The statutory tax year is the calendar year, running 1 January to 31 December (Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 7). Social insurance contributions follow the same calendar basis.
Can employers adopt an alternate tax year? If yes, under what circumstances and what are the implications?
Yes, in limited circumstances. The law permits a 12-month overlapping fiscal year to be used as the basis of assessment, and it allows a shorter or longer assessment period (maximum 18 months) where a business starts up, ceases, or changes its year-end, or on death or bankruptcy of the taxpayer (Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 7).
* A normal ongoing employer should use the calendar year for salaries tax.
* Where a short or long period applies, the personal exemption is pro-rated on a 30-day-month basis (days divided by 30) rather than granted in full (Executive Regulations Art. 52).
* The monthly Form 4 withholding cycle is unaffected: withholding, filing and payment remain monthly regardless of the entity's chosen year-end.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 7; Executive Regulations Art. 52.
What is the official currency for payroll processing and reporting? Provide the ISO code.
The official currency is the Yemeni Rial, ISO code YER. Wages must be paid in legal tender (Yemeni currency) (Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 61). All statutory reporting to the Tax Authority and the General Corporation for Social Security (GCSS) is denominated in YER.
Because two rival central banks quote very different rates for the same currency (Aden approximately YER 1,600 per USD; Sana'a approximately YER 535 per USD as at December 2025), payroll should be run and reported in YER only. If a USD equivalent is unavoidable, state which market rate was applied.
Legal basis: Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 61.
What is the official language for statutory reports and employee payslips?
The official language for statutory filings is Arabic. The salaries tax return (Form 4) and its attachments are Arabic-language documents filed with the Tax Authority, and the GCSS contribution schedule is likewise in Arabic. The Labour Law requires the employer to issue a wage document (payslip) detailing gross wage, deductions and net pay (Art. 66); it does not prescribe the language, but the statutory-facing version should be Arabic. A bilingual (Arabic/English) payslip is acceptable for practical use provided the statutory content is present in Arabic.
Legal basis: Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 66; Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 25 (Form 4).

Describe the statutory rule for handling pay dates that fall on a weekend or public holiday.
The Labour Law requires wages to be paid on a working day (Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 61). Where a statutory pay date falls on the weekend (Friday–Saturday) or a public holiday, payment should be brought forward to the preceding working day so the worker is not paid late, since the Art. 61 deadlines (6th of the month for monthly pay, 3rd after each half-month) are latest dates, not target dates.
* No specific statute addresses tax or GCSS remittance dates falling on a non-working day. The prudent administrative practice is to file and pay on the last working day before the deadline, so the 10-day window is not missed.
* Late filing of Form 4 attracts a delay penalty of 2% of the tax due per month or part-month, capped at the tax due (Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 155).
Legal basis: Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 61; Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 155.
What are the common payroll pay dates and cut-off dates for payroll processing?
Monthly payroll is standard. Common practice is to set the pay date at or before the 6th of the following month, which is the statutory backstop for monthly wages (Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 61(1)). The Labour Law does not fix a processing cut-off date; cut-offs are an employer choice driven by the pay date and the 10-day tax deadline.

1.3 Extra Pay Period Handling
Describe the statutory handling and calculation adjustments required for fiscal years containing extra pay periods (e.g., 53 weekly pays).
Not Applicable in the statutory sense — Yemen taxes salaries on a monthly withholding basis, not a per-pay-period annualisation, so there is no statutory "53rd period" adjustment. For applying the Labour Law and the tax pro-rating rules, a year is treated as 365 days and a month as 30 days (Labour Law Art. 9; Executive Regulations Art. 52). A weekly-paid worker who receives an extra (53rd) weekly payment in a calendar year is simply taxed on the actual pay that falls within each monthly Form 4 period; the extra payment is captured in whichever month it is paid, with no separate annualisation correction.
Legal basis: Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 9; Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 7; Executive Regulations Art. 52.
Detail the impact on annualized tax calculations and social security contributions.
There is no annualised true-up in the Yemeni salaries regime. Salaries tax is a final monthly withholding via Form 4; the personal exemption of YER 10,000 is applied per month, and the progressive 0/10/15% bands are applied to each month's taxable pay.
* Income tax: an extra weekly payment increases the taxable pay of the month in which it is paid. That month's exemption and bands apply as normal; no annual reconciliation return exists to redistribute it (year-end is effectively the sum of the monthly returns).
* Social insurance: contributions (employee 6%, employer 9%) are computed on the covered earnings actually paid in the month. An extra weekly wage raises that month's covered earnings and contributions proportionally; no ceiling to breach has been located (see the administrative caveat below).
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 36, Art. 48; Executive Regulations Art. 52; Social Insurance Law 1991.
If optional adjustment methods are permitted, provide a step-by-step guide.
No optional annualisation or averaging method is prescribed by Yemeni statute. The compliant approach for any month containing an extra weekly payment is:
1. Aggregate all wages actually paid in the calendar month (regular weeks plus the extra week).
2. Compute social insurance on the month's covered earnings (basic plus allowances, excluding bonuses and overtime): employee 6% deducted, employer 9% on-cost.
3. Deduct the employee SI contribution and any within-cap job-expense allowances to reach taxable income.
4. Apply the single monthly personal exemption (YER 10,000) once, then the 0/10/15% bands, to that month's taxable salary; tax any "other income" (bonus, overtime) at the flat 15%.
5. File and pay on the Form 4 for that month within the first 10 days of the following month.
The exemption is granted once per month only and is not multiplied because an extra weekly pay occurred (Executive Regulations Art. 52).
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 36, Art. 48; Executive Regulations Art. 52.

Are there any regional, state-specific, or company-specific holidays?
There are no formal state-specific statutory holidays in the payroll sense, but observance can differ by zone: the Sana'a authority may not emphasise the same national days as the Aden Government, and the two administrations may sight the Islamic new moon independently, shifting Eid and other Islamic dates by about a day. Company-specific holidays are not regulated by the Labour Law; an employer may grant additional paid days as a contractual or policy benefit over and above the statutory minimum.
Legal basis: Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 78.
1.5 Rounding and Precision Rules
What are the statutory or common business practice rules for rounding final net pay and individual calculation components?
The Income Tax Law and its Executive Regulations prescribe no explicit monetary rounding rule for salaries tax. The best-supported position is to compute in whole Yemeni Rial, which is standard business practice for a currency with no circulating sub-unit in payroll use. Period apportionment (for a partial month, exemption pro-rating, or overtime and deduction caps) uses a 30-day month and a 365-day year convention (Executive Regulations Art. 52; Labour Law Art. 9). Employers should apply whole-rial rounding consistently to each component. (No statutory rounding rule was located; confirm any specific administrative rounding convention with the Tax Authority for a given employer.)
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 36; Executive Regulations Art. 52; Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 9.
What level of decimal precision is required for statutory reporting?
No decimal precision is mandated for salaries tax reporting in the primary sources. Form 4 records amounts in whole Yemeni Rial, and the practical reporting standard is whole-rial figures for total amounts, excluded/deducted amounts, taxable income and tax due. Contributions to GCSS should likewise be reported in whole rial. Sub-rial decimals are not used in Yemeni payroll statutory reporting.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 25 (Form 4); Executive Regulations Art. 52.

2.2 Employee Information
What are the different residential statuses for an individual?
An individual is either resident or non-resident for salaries tax. A natural person is resident if any one of the following applies (Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 5):
* Has a permanent place of residence in the Republic; or
* Is present in the Republic for at least 183 days (continuous or intermittent) within a 12-month period ending in the tax year (where there is no permanent home); or
* Is a Yemeni working abroad who receives his employment income from the Republic.
A person meeting none of these tests is non-resident. Residency drives the rate: residents receive the personal exemption and the progressive 0/10/15% schedule; non-residents are taxed at a flat 20% with no exemption.

What previous employment information must new joiners declare?
The primary sources do not prescribe a formal statutory "starter declaration" of prior employment for Yemeni salaries tax (there is no P45/P60 equivalent, and salaries tax is a final monthly withholding with a per-month exemption rather than a cumulative annual code). The practical requirements at onboarding are:
* Insurance and pension enrolment status: whether the joiner is already registered with GCSS and their existing social-security number, so the employer's new-insured-employee statement (a Form 4 attachment) is correct.
* The employment contract, which is filed with Form 4 for newly enrolled employees.
* For foreign hires: nationality and residency status, feeding the Form 4 foreign-labour statement and the resident/non-resident split.
Because the monthly exemption is granted once per month by the current employer, no prior-employer year-to-date pay figure is needed for tax purposes. (No statutory prior-employment declaration form was located; onboarding data should be captured to support the Form 4 attachments and GCSS enrolment.)
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 25 (Form 4 attachments), Art. 36; Social Insurance Law 1991.
2.3 Data Management and Retention
What are the statutory requirements for payroll record retention (e.g., number of years)?
The Income Tax Law imposes a bookkeeping and record-production obligation: taxpayers must keep proper books and records and produce salary, wage and bonus data on the Authority's request so that the salaries tax paid can be verified. Failure to keep proper books is penalised (Income Tax Law 17/2010; the books-and-records penalty is a lump-sum fine of YER 1,000,000 for large and YER 300,000 for medium taxpayers). The sources do not state an explicit fixed number of retention years for payroll records. The best-supported position is to retain payroll and salaries tax records for the period over which the Authority may assess or reopen a return, and to keep them available for on-demand production. (No numeric retention period is fixed in the primary sources; align retention to the tax assessment/limitation window and confirm with the Tax Authority.)
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 154 (books-and-records penalty), Art. 25; Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 66 (wage documents).
Are there specific data privacy laws (like GDPR) that govern the storage and handling of employee payroll data?
Not Applicable in the GDPR sense — Yemen has no comprehensive, GDPR-style data-protection statute governing employee payroll data. The only relevant obligations are the tax-law duty to keep and confidentially handle records for verification, and the general labour-law duty to issue accurate wage documents. Employers handling Yemeni payroll data across borders should apply their own group data-protection standards, since no local omnibus privacy law imposes storage, consent or transfer rules.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 25 (record obligation); Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 66.
How should historical payroll data (e.g., from a previous provider) be handled for cumulative calculations?
Yemeni salaries tax is a non-cumulative, final monthly withholding: the personal exemption and the 0/10/15% bands are applied per month, so there is no year-to-date cumulation of prior-provider pay for tax purposes. Historical data is therefore handled as reference and continuity data rather than as an input to a cumulative tax recalculation.
* Load prior-period data for statutory continuity: existing TIN and GCSS numbers, contract dates, and prior social-insurance enrolment, so the current provider's Form 4 and GCSS filings are correct.
* Retain historical payroll records to meet the tax record-production obligation and to support any audit or reassessment.
* Do not carry a year-to-date taxable pay or exemption balance across providers; each month stands alone for salaries tax, and social insurance is computed on the month's covered earnings.
* End-of-service gratuity accrual depends on total years of service (at least one month's last wage per year, for workers outside social insurance, Labour Law Art. 120); prior service history must therefore be preserved even though it does not affect monthly tax cumulation.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 36, Art. 48 (per-month basis); Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 120; Social Insurance Law 1991.

Module 3: The Gross-to-Net Calculation Framework
This module sets out the end-to-end sequence Mercans applies to convert an employee's gross earnings into net take-home pay under Yemeni law, together with the employer on-costs that run alongside it. The framework combines the Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 (as amended), the Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 and its Executive Regulations, and the Social Insurance Law of 1991. All amounts are expressed in Yemeni Rial (YER), the mandatory currency for wage payment (Labour Law Art. 61).
3.1 Gross-to-Net Flow
The calculation follows a fixed order. Social insurance is settled before income tax because the employee's statutory social-insurance contribution is deductible from taxable income (Income Tax Law Art. 49; Exec Regs Art. 57). Income tax itself is split into two parallel computations — a progressive charge on regular salary and a flat charge on "other income". Post-tax deductions are applied last. The employer's own social-insurance cost is computed in parallel and never reduces the employee's net pay.

Step 1: Summation of Gross Earnings
The starting point is total gross earnings for the pay period. This is the sum of the basic wage, all cash allowances (for example housing, transport, representation, hazard, specialization), the assessed money value of any taxable benefits in kind, and any variable pay such as bonuses, overtime and commissions (Income Tax Law Art. 48; Exec Regs Arts. 55-56). Gross earnings are the widest figure in the calculation; later steps carve out amounts that are exempt from tax, excluded from the social-insurance base, or both. Benefits in kind are valued according to Exec Regs Art. 56 (for example in-kind housing at its estimated rental value) and added to gross for tax purposes only.
Step 2: Determination of Social Insurance Base and Employee Contribution
The social-insurance base ("covered earnings") is the basic salary plus all allowances, excluding bonuses and overtime (SSA SSPTW 2018; Social Insurance Law 1991). In the reviewed payroll the base is applied as a fixed insured wage - the contractual basic plus housing - with transport, bonuses and overtime excluded. The employee's contribution is 6% of covered earnings and is withheld from pay. No statutory ceiling or floor on covered earnings has been located, so contributions are computed on full covered earnings; any employer-specific ceiling should be confirmed with the General Corporation for Social Security (GCSS). The employee's 6% contribution is important beyond social insurance itself, because it is deducted before income tax is calculated in Step 3.
Step 3: Determination of Taxable Income
Taxable income is gross taxable earnings less the amounts the law removes before applying the rate schedule. The deductions are: the employee's statutory social-insurance contribution from Step 2 (Income Tax Law Art. 49; Exec Regs Art. 57); job-expense allowances within their statutory caps — transport up to YER 30,000/month and representation-plus-appearance up to YER 35,000/month, with any excess remaining taxable (Exec Regs Art. 57); and other exempt items such as end-of-service entitlements, work-injury compensation and reimbursed travel or per-diems (Income Tax Law Art. 50). What remains is the amount that enters the tax computation.
Step 4: Calculation of Income Tax Withholding
The taxable amount is split into two streams that are taxed differently (Income Tax Law Arts. 48-49; Exec Regs Art. 55; Form 4). Regular monthly salary and its statutory allowances are taxed on the progressive resident schedule after the YER 10,000 monthly personal exemption: 0% on the first 10,000, 10% on the next 10,000 (from 10,000 to 20,000), and 15% on everything above 20,000 (Income Tax Law Arts. 36 and 48). "Other income" of the salary earner — bonuses, overtime, session or board allowances, incentives and commissions — is taxed at a flat 15% on the gross with no exemption (Income Tax Law Art. 49; Exec Regs Art. 55(b); Form 4 row 2). A non-resident employee is taxed instead at a flat 20% with no exemption on the salary stream (Income Tax Law Art. 37; Form 4). The two tax figures are summed to give the monthly withholding.

	Step 5: Application of Post-Tax Deductions
Deductions that are not tax-relieved are applied after income tax. Yemeni law tightly restricts these: wages may be attached only under a final court judgment (Labour Law Art. 63), and any monthly instalment recovered for damage or loss caused by the worker's fault may not exceed 25% of the basic wage (Labour Law Art. 64). Voluntary private savings are not pre-tax and, where deducted, also fall in this post-tax layer. Only the statutory social-insurance, pension and health contributions receive pre-tax treatment (Income Tax Law Art. 49).
Step 6: Finalization of Net Pay
Net pay is the gross cash earnings less the employee social-insurance contribution, less the income tax withheld, less any post-tax deductions:
Net pay = gross cash earnings − employee SI (6%) − income tax − post-tax deductions.
The employer must issue a payslip detailing the wage, the deductions made and the net wage paid (Labour Law Art. 66). Monthly-paid wages are due no later than the 6th day of the following month (Labour Law Art. 61).
Employer On-Costs (Calculated in Parallel)
The employer's social-insurance contribution is 9% of the same covered-earnings base used in Step 2 (SSA SSPTW 2018; Social Insurance Law 1991). This is an employer cost that runs in parallel with the employee calculation; it does not reduce the employee's net pay. A possible work-injury add-on is noted as an administrative caveat rather than a separate confirmed rate, and some sources cite ~11% (likely public-sector); the private-sector employer rate applied here is 9%. The employer's total cost of employment is the gross cash plus the employer social-insurance contribution.
Employer total cost = gross cash earnings + employer SI (9%).

Module 4: Gross Compensation
This module describes how gross compensation is built up under Yemeni law: the minimum wage, the salary components that make up pay, how fixed pay is prorated, and the statutory rules for overtime, bonuses and wage classification. All amounts are in Yemeni Rial (YER). The governing statutes are the Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 (as amended by Laws 25/1997, 11/2001 and 25/2003), the Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 with its Executive Regulations, and the Social Insurance Law of 1991. Yemen is administratively split between the Aden and Sana'a authorities, but both apply the same pre-war statutory framework, so the rules below are uniform in law; the practical divergence is in exchange rates and administration, not in the statutes.
4.1 Minimum Wage
Is there a minimum wage? Who is eligible?
Yemen has a statutory minimum wage. The Labour Law does not fix a figure directly; instead it sets a mechanism, providing that a worker's wage may not be less than the minimum wage payable in the State administrative apparatus, that is the civil service (Labour Law Art. 55(1)). The floor applies to workers covered by the Labour Law — private-sector employees — and is currently understood to be YER 21,000 per month, the civil-service minimum. For piece-rate or production workers, the average daily wage may not fall below the minimum daily wage set for the profession or trade (Labour Law Art. 55(2)). Workers outside the Labour Law's scope (civil servants, domestic servants, casual and family workers, certain agricultural workers) are governed by their own regimes (Labour Law Art. 3).
Is the minimum wage applicable at a national level or does it vary by province/state?
The minimum wage is national. It is a single statutory floor pinned to the civil-service minimum and does not vary by province or governorate. Because the same YER figure has very different purchasing power in the Aden and Sana'a zones (owing to the split exchange-rate reality), the real value differs by zone, but the nominal statutory figure is the same country-wide (Labour Law Art. 55).
Provide all applicable minimum wage rates.
There is a single applicable rate. It has not been formally updated since around 2013, and no 2026 decree revising it was located for either administration.

	Does the minimum wage impact any tax or social security calculations?
No. The minimum wage sets the lowest lawful gross wage, but it does not alter tax or social-insurance mechanics. The personal income-tax exemption is a separate figure of YER 10,000 per month (Income Tax Law Art. 36; Exec Regs Art. 52), and social-insurance contributions are computed on covered earnings at 6% employee and 9% employer regardless of the minimum-wage level (Social Insurance Law 1991). A worker earning the minimum wage of YER 21,000 would have a taxable amount above the YER 10,000 exemption and would therefore still bear a small income-tax and social-insurance charge; the minimum wage neither raises the exemption nor creates a contribution floor.
How are deductions handled if they cannot be made due to minimum wage constraints?
The Labour Law does not set an explicit "net pay cannot fall below the minimum wage" protection, but it constrains deductions in ways that operate as protection. Statutory social insurance and income tax are mandatory and are withheld regardless. Discretionary or recovery deductions are capped: recovery of damage or loss caused by the worker may not exceed 25% of the basic wage in any month (Labour Law Art. 64), and wage attachment is permitted only under a final court judgment (Labour Law Art. 63). Where these caps prevent full recovery in a period, the recoverable balance carries forward to later periods within the same 25%-of-basic-wage monthly limit. The worker's wage is also a first-priority claim over the employer's assets (Labour Law Art. 8). In practice, therefore, only mandatory statutory withholdings and capped recoveries reduce a minimum-wage earner's pay, and voluntary deductions should be suspended before the statutory limits are breached.
Legal basis: Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 Arts. 3, 8, 55, 63, 64; Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 Art. 36 and Exec Regs Art. 52; Social Insurance Law of 1991.
4.2 Salary Components
What are the mandatory and common salary components?
The mandatory core is the basic wage (الأجر الأساسي), the pay for the work itself, which every contract must define and on which minimum-wage, overtime and deduction-cap calculations run (Labour Law Art. 2). Beyond the basic wage, the common components are cash allowances — most frequently housing, transport/commuting, representation and appearance, expatriation or field/site, specialization or dedication, hazard, and judiciary allowances — together with variable pay such as overtime, bonuses, incentives and commissions (Exec Regs Arts. 55-56). Certain statutory premiums are payable when their conditions are met: a night-work allowance of +15% of the basic wage and a shift-work allowance of +10% where the ordinary work is at night or on rotating shifts for more than 10 days in the month (Labour Law Art. 57).
What is the general definition and pay cycle for each component?
The basic wage is the fixed periodic remuneration for the job, paid on the contractual cycle. Cash allowances are recurring additions to the basic wage that meet a defined need (housing, transport, and so on) and are normally paid on the same monthly cycle. Overtime is pay for hours worked beyond normal hours, settled with the pay period in which it is earned. Bonuses and incentives are variable amounts paid periodically or on an event basis (annual, performance, session/board). The default wage cycle is monthly, payable no later than the 6th day of the following month; half-monthly pay is due within 3 days of each half-month, and hourly, daily and weekly workers must be paid at least weekly (Labour Law Art. 61).
Which components are taxable vs non-taxable and sociable vs non-sociable?
The classification below reconciles the income-tax treatment (Income Tax Law Arts. 48-50; Exec Regs Arts. 55-57) with the social-insurance base (basic salary plus all allowances, excluding bonuses and overtime).
Are components uniform across the country or do they differ by state/province?
The components and their statutory treatment are uniform nationally. Both the Aden and Sana'a administrations apply the same Labour Law, Income Tax Law and Social Insurance Law, so the definition, taxability and sociability of each component are identical in law across the country. The only material difference between zones is the real (USD-equivalent) value of a given YER amount because of the split exchange rate; that is a currency effect, not a difference in component rules.
What is the calculation logic for each salary component?
The basic wage is a fixed contractual figure. Cash allowances are either fixed amounts or a percentage of the basic wage as set in the contract or policy; the transport and representation/appearance allowances are relieved from tax only up to their statutory caps (YER 30,000 and YER 35,000 per month), with any excess taxable (Exec Regs Art. 57). Overtime is derived from the basic hourly wage at 150% on ordinary days and 200% on nights, weekly rest days and official holidays (Labour Law Art. 56); the basic hourly wage is the basic wage reduced to an hourly figure using the 8-hour day / 48-hour week and 30-day month conventions (Labour Law Arts. 9, 71). The night and shift allowances are +15% and +10% of the basic wage respectively (Labour Law Art. 57). Benefits in kind are valued under Exec Regs Art. 56 (for example in-kind housing at estimated rental value) for tax purposes. Employee social insurance is 6% of covered earnings; income tax follows the Module 3 flow.
Which components require employee-declared values and submissions?
Payroll components are primarily employer-set from the contract and policy rather than employee-declared. The employee-driven inputs that affect the calculation are the data needed to establish status and entitlements: residency status (resident vs non-resident under Income Tax Law Art. 5), which changes the tax basis; nationality and contract documentation for the Form 4 attachments (statements of new insured employees, terminated employees and foreign labour, with contracts); and, where relevant, documentation supporting exempt reimbursements such as travel and per-diems, and medical certification for sick leave (Labour Law Art. 81). There is no self-assessment tax-declaration mechanism for the employee at the payroll level — salaries tax is operated as a final monthly withholding by the employer via Form 4.

Both the 6% employee and 9% employer contributions are computed on this base. No statutory floor or ceiling on covered earnings has been located; contributions are therefore computed on full covered earnings, and any employer-specific ceiling should be confirmed with the GCSS.

4.3 Proration
Are fixed wages prorated for the number of working days?
Yes. When an employee is present for only part of a pay period — a mid-period hire or a mid-period termination — fixed salary components are prorated to the portion of the period worked. The Labour Law establishes a 30-day-month convention for applying the Code (Labour Law Art. 9), which provides the standard divisor, and the Executive Regulations use the same 30-day basis for pro-rating the personal tax exemption (Exec Regs Art. 52). Notice pay and its equivalents are likewise computed on a 30-day basis (Labour Law Art. 38(4)).
Describe the statutory or standard business practice for prorating fixed salary components for mid-period hires and terminations.
The standard practice is to pay the fixed monthly salary in proportion to calendar days of employment within the month, using a fixed 30-day month regardless of the actual number of days in the calendar month (Labour Law Art. 9). For a mid-period hire, the count runs from the first day of employment to month-end; for a mid-period termination, it runs from the first of the month to the last day of service, with final wages due the day after contract end (or within 6 days for voluntary resignation) (Labour Law Art. 65). Fixed cash allowances are prorated on the same basis as the basic wage. The personal tax exemption is prorated on the same 30-day convention (Exec Regs Art. 52), and social-insurance contributions follow the prorated covered earnings.
Provide the proration formula and a worked example.
The proration formula, using the 30-day-month convention, is:
Prorated pay = full monthly fixed pay × (days employed in the month ÷ 30).

4.4 Overtime
Detail the statutory rules for overtime, including eligibility criteria and mandatory rates.
Overtime is work beyond the normal hours of 8 per day / 48 per week (36 per week in Ramadan), and it may be compensated either in money or in paid time off (Labour Law Arts. 71, 56, 75). Total working hours including overtime may not exceed 12 hours in a day (Labour Law Art. 74(2)). All premiums are calculated on the basic wage (Labour Law Art. 2). Cash overtime is paid at 150% of the basic hourly wage on ordinary working days, and at 200% of the basic hourly wage on nights, the weekly rest day (Friday) and official holidays, in addition to whatever is otherwise due for that holiday (Labour Law Art. 56). As an alternative to cash, the employer may grant compensating paid rest — 1.5 times for ordinary-day overtime, 2 times for additional night overtime, and equivalent time (granted within one month) for weekly-rest-day and holiday work (Labour Law Art. 75). Separate from overtime, a regular night-work allowance of +15% of the basic wage and a shift-work allowance of +10% apply where the ordinary work is at night or on rotating shifts for more than 10 days in the month; these two allowances cannot be combined (Labour Law Art. 57).

	Explain how overtime is treated for income tax and social security calculations.
For income tax, overtime is "other income" of the salary earner and is taxed at a flat 15% on the gross with no personal exemption applied (Income Tax Law Art. 49; Exec Regs Art. 55(b); Form 4 row 2). For social insurance, overtime is excluded from covered earnings, so neither the 6% employee nor the 9% employer contribution is charged on it (SSA SSPTW 2018). 4.5 Bonuses
Is there a statutory requirement to pay bonuses? If so, how are they calculated?
There is no general statutory requirement to pay a discretionary or annual bonus under the Labour Law. Bonuses, incentives and gratuities are contractual or policy-based, and where an employer pays them, the amount and calculation are set by the contract, collective terms or policy rather than by statute. This is distinct from the mandatory statutory entitlements that are sometimes loosely called "bonuses" — for example paid annual leave, sick pay and the end-of-service gratuity — which are governed by their own articles (Labour Law Arts. 79, 80, 120). Where a bonus is contractually promised, it becomes a due entitlement payable on its agreed terms.
Is there a specific tax treatment for bonuses?
Yes. Bonuses are "other income" of the salary earner and are taxed at a flat 15% on the gross with no personal exemption, separately from the progressive charge on regular salary (Income Tax Law Art. 49; Exec Regs Art. 55(b); Form 4 row 2). Bonuses are also excluded from the social-insurance base, so no employee or employer social-insurance contribution is charged on them (SSA SSPTW 2018). Worked example (canonical Example C): a monthly bonus of YER 100,000 paid on top of the Example A salary is taxed at 15% × 100,000 = YER 15,000, and the bonus is not part of the SI base, so it attracts no social-insurance contribution.
Legal basis: Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 Arts. 79, 80, 120; Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 Art. 49 and Exec Regs Art. 55(b); Social Insurance Law of 1991.
4.6 Wage Classification (Country-Specific)
Are there country-specific classifications for wages critical for social security or tax calculations?
Yes. Yemeni law draws a critical distinction between the basic wage and the full wage (Labour Law Art. 2), and the tax and social-insurance systems each define their own base on top of that distinction. The basic wage drives overtime premiums, the damage-recovery deduction cap and the minimum-wage comparison. The social-insurance base is a third concept — basic salary plus all allowances but excluding bonuses and overtime — while the income-tax base is the taxable full wage less statutory reliefs. Getting each classification right is essential because the same gross figure produces different bases for overtime, social insurance and tax.
Provide a detailed definition for each classification.
The basic wage (الأجر الأساسي) is what the employer pays the worker for the work itself — in cash or in-kind valued in money — excluding all other entitlements of any kind (Labour Law Art. 2). It is the reference figure for calculating overtime premiums (Art. 56), the night/shift allowances (Art. 57) and the 25% damage-recovery deduction cap (Art. 64), and it is the figure compared against the minimum wage.
The full (total) wage (الأجر الكامل) is what the employer pays for the work plus all other entitlements of any kind — that is, the basic wage plus all allowances and money-valued benefits (Labour Law Art. 2).
The social-insurance base is the basic salary plus all allowances, excluding bonuses and overtime, on which the 6%/9% contributions are charged (SSA SSPTW 2018; Social Insurance Law 1991).
The income-tax base is the taxable full wage (including taxable benefits in kind) less the employee statutory contributions, less job-expense allowances within their caps and other exempt items, taxed on the progressive resident schedule for regular salary and at a flat 15% for other income (Income Tax Law Arts. 48-50; Exec Regs Arts. 55-57).

Module 5: Social Security & Pension Systems
Yemen operates a single, national social-insurance system. The governing statute is the Republican Decree Law Concerning Social Insurance of 1991, which remains the framework in force in 2026 and is recognised in both the Aden-controlled and Sana'a-controlled zones. The divergence between the two administrations is administrative (two rival funds, delayed payments, fragmented governance), not statutory — there is no verified separate Sana'a social-insurance law. This module states contribution rules for private-sector employees, who are the population in scope (civil servants, judicial, diplomatic, military, domestic, casual, family and excluded agricultural workers are out of scope).
All amounts are stated in Yemeni Rial (YER). Because the same YER figure carries very different USD value by zone (Aden ≈ YER 1,600/USD; Sana'a ≈ YER 535/USD, Dec 2025), the module works entirely in YER.

Are contributions based on actual or standard salary?
Contributions are based on actual covered earnings for the period — the insured wage: in the reviewed payroll a stable contractual figure (basic plus housing) that excludes transport, bonuses and overtime and does not move with the month's paid basic (retro and proration adjustments do not enter the SI base).
Describe rules for eligibility, joining month, termination month, mid-period hires, mid-period terminations, and mid-period salary changes.
* Eligibility: private-sector employees under an employment relationship are covered from the start of employment; the employer must enrol new insured employees (Form 4 requires a statement of newly enrolled employees plus their contracts). Excluded categories (civil servants, judicial, diplomatic, military, domestic, casual/daily-wage, family, excluded agricultural workers) are not in the private-sector scheme.
* Joining month: contributions begin in the month employment starts. For a mid-month start, pro-rate covered earnings for the days worked using a 30-day month (days worked ÷ 30 × monthly covered earnings), then apply 6%/9%. The sources do not prescribe a separate rounding rule; compute in whole rials.
* Termination month: contributions run through the last month of covered employment, pro-rated for a mid-month leaver on the same 30-day basis.
* Mid-period hires / terminations: treat as above — pro-rate the covered base to the covered days, then apply the flat rates. No separate minimum applies.
* Mid-period salary changes: contributions follow actual earnings. If pay changes mid-month, apply each rate to the earnings actually paid for the relevant portion of the period; the simplest compliant approach is to compute on the total covered earnings actually paid for the month.
No statutory ceiling or floor caps these pro-rated figures (administrative gap — confirm with GCSS).
Do different payment frequencies affect contribution rates or parameters?
No. The 6%/9% rates and the covered-earnings base are frequency-independent. Whether the employee is paid monthly, half-monthly, weekly or daily, the contribution is the same percentage of covered earnings for the covered period. Frequency affects only when wages are paid (Labour Law Art. 61: monthly by the 6th of the following month; half-monthly by the 3rd; hourly/daily/weekly at least weekly), not how much is contributed.
Detail variations based on employee age or gender.
There are no age-based or gender-based variations in contribution rates or the base. The 6%/9% split applies uniformly regardless of the employee's age or gender. Age becomes relevant only for benefit eligibility (e.g. reaching retirement age to draw an old-age pension), which is a benefits question, not a contribution-rate question. Gender is payroll-neutral for contributions; equal-pay protection (Labour Law Art. 67) reinforces that the base is computed identically.
Is there a minimum number of working days required for contributions?
No minimum number of working days for social-insurance coverage was located in the primary sources. Contributions attach to covered earnings from the start of the employment relationship. (Contrast with income tax, where daily-wage workers not settled with one employer for more than 15 days in a month are exempt — that 15-day rule is a tax-exemption test for daily-wage workers, not a social-insurance threshold, and daily/casual workers are in any event outside the private-sector scheme's core payroll population.) Absent a published minimum-days rule, treat any covered employment in a month as generating a pro-rated contribution.
How are contributions calculated for Board of Directors who are also employees?
Where a board member is also a salaried employee under an employment relationship, social insurance is computed on their covered employment earnings (basic + allowances) exactly as for any employee — 6% employee / 9% employer on that covered base. Board or session allowances, board bonuses and directors' fees are excluded from the covered-earnings base (they are bonuses/allowances of the board type, which the SI base strips out). So a director-employee contributes on their salary, but not on their board sitting fees. (For tax, those board/session amounts are "other income" taxed at a flat 15% — see Module 6.)
Are year-end adjustments required?
No year-end reconciliation of social-insurance contributions is required in the located sources. Contributions are operated as a monthly determination on actual covered earnings and remitted on the monthly GCSS schedule; the year is effectively the sum of the monthly contributions. There is no annual true-up filing. If covered earnings are mis-stated in a month, correct it via the ordinary monthly schedule for the affected period rather than an annual adjustment. (The exact GCSS return format is not in the source set — flag as an administrative gap, not a verified form.)
Legal basis: Social Insurance Law of 1991; SSA SSPTW — Yemen (2018); Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 Arts. 9, 61, 67; Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 Art. 49 (SI pre-tax); FACTS_BASE §1, §3, §5, §8.
5.3 Voluntary Contributions & Private Schemes
Can employees and employers make additional voluntary contributions?
The primary sources do not establish a statutory voluntary top-up mechanism within the mandatory GCSS scheme for ordinary private-sector employees. The mandatory 6%/9% is the located regime. Two adjacent points are supported: (i) Labour Law Art. 120 recognises more-favourable special schemes — an employer may operate an end-of-service or pension arrangement more generous than the statutory minimum, and where its terms are better for the worker it governs; and (ii) employers may establish pension, savings or end-of-service funds. So additional contributions are possible through an employer-established scheme rather than as voluntary buy-in to GCSS. Treat any voluntary GCSS top-up as unconfirmed and to be checked with the fund.
Which employee contributions are allowed as pre-tax deductions?
Only statutory contributions are pre-tax. Income Tax Law Art. 49 (with Exec Regs Art. 57) excludes from taxable salary the amounts set aside for the employee's pension/retirement contribution, social-insurance premium and health-insurance premium under the applicable laws. Accordingly:

Module 6: Income Tax Withholding System
Yemen taxes employment income through the salaries and wages tax (ضريبة المرتبات والأجور), a Pay-As-You-Earn withholding operated by the employer under Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 and its Executive Regulations. The law remains in force in 2026 across both administrations; the divergence between Aden and Sana'a is administrative (two tax authorities, separate collection), not statutory. All figures are in Yemeni Rial (YER); the tax year is the calendar year, 1 January – 31 December (Law Art. 7).
6.1 System Overview and Methodology
The withholding system distinguishes three charging tracks, and the correct track depends first on residency and then on the type of income. Describe the country's income tax withholding system.
The employer is the withholding agent. Each pay period the employer computes the salaries tax due on every employee's taxable employment income, deducts it at source, and remits it to the Tax Authority (a branch, the Central Bank, or an authorised bank) with the monthly Form 4 salaries-tax return. It is a final withholding operated monthly: there is no separate annual employee-level reconciliation return (P60 equivalent) in the primary sources — the year is the sum of the monthly returns. Residents are taxed progressively after a personal exemption; non-residents are taxed at a flat rate with no exemption; and variable pay ("other income") is taxed at a flat 15% for residents with no exemption.
What types of taxes are withheld by the employer?
The employer withholds the salaries and wages tax on: (i) regular salary and statutory allowances (progressive for residents; flat 20% for non-residents); (ii) "other income" of salary earners — bonuses, overtime, incentives, session/board allowances, commissions, grants, profit share (flat 15% for residents, no exemption); and (iii) the value of taxable benefits in kind. The employer also withholds and remits the employee's statutory social-insurance contribution (6%) — see Module 5 — but that is a social-insurance deduction, not a tax. A separate 10% withholding on certain service/commission/brokerage payments (remitted within 15 days) can arise but is outside the salaries-tax/PAYE stream.
At what level are tax rates published (central or state)?
Tax rates are set centrally by national statute — Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 and its Executive Regulations, administered by the Ministry of Finance / Tax Authority. There are no sub-national or state salary-tax rates. The only practical variation is administrative collection: the Aden and Sana'a tax authorities each administer the same central rates in their zone.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 Arts. 3, 5, 7, 8, 25, 37, 48, 49, 50; Executive Regulations Arts. 52, 54, 55, 56, 57, 151; Form 4; FACTS_BASE §1, §2, controls B1/C2.
6.1.1 Taxable Income Definition
Provide the statutory definition of 'Taxable Income'.
Salaries tax applies to salaries, wages and the like, including cash and in-kind benefits and advantages (Law Art. 48). This covers all amounts paid to an employee arising from work for another — by contract or without, whatever the label, form or reason, whether the work is performed inside or outside the Republic, so long as the source is the Republic — plus amounts from a foreign source for work performed in the Republic, and salaries/bonuses/allowances to chairmen and members of boards and managers of companies (Law Art. 48(a)–(d); source rule Art. 3). From this gross, the law excludes (Law Art. 49; Exec Regs Art. 57): the employee's statutory pension/social-insurance/health-insurance contributions; and job-expense reimbursements within caps (representation + appearance ≤ YER 35,000/month; transport ≤ YER 30,000/month; travel tickets, per-diems and relocation costs on appointment/transfer/exit). Taxable income is the gross salary income minus these exclusions.
Explain how this base is derived from total gross earnings.
The derivation for a resident, per pay period:
11. Total gross earnings = basic salary + all cash allowances + value of taxable benefits in kind (Exec Regs Art. 56) + any variable pay.
12. Less employee statutory social-insurance contribution (6% of covered earnings) — pre-tax (Art. 49).
13. Less job-expense allowances within caps: transport up to YER 30,000/month; representation + appearance up to YER 35,000/month; any excess above a cap is taxable as other income at the flat 15% rate (Row 2), not on the resident salary table, and is excluded from the social-insurance base [g][h](Exec Regs Art. 57).
14. Less other exempt items (statutory end-of-service, pensions, work-injury compensation, documented medical aid) — Art. 50.
15. The remainder is taxable income, which is then split: (a) regular monthly salary + statutory allowances → progressive 0/10/15% after the YER 10,000 monthly exemption; (b) "other income" (bonus, overtime, incentives, board/session, commissions) → flat 15% with no exemption.
Period apportionment uses a 30-day month (Exec Regs Art. 52; Labour Law Art. 9). No explicit monetary rounding rule is prescribed for salaries tax; standard practice is to compute in whole rials (administrative caveat, confirm against a clean statute copy).

Resident, regular salary (monthly):
* Taxable salary T = (gross taxable salary) − (employee SI 6%) − (allowances within caps).
* Tax = 0% × min(T, 10,000) + 10% × max(0, min(T, 20,000) − 10,000) + 15% × max(0, T − 20,000).
* In words: first YER 10,000 free; next YER 10,000 (the 10,000–20,000 band) at 10%; everything above YER 20,000 at 15%.

How does tax applicability and calculation logic differ by residential status?
* Resident: progressive 0/10/15% on regular salary after the YER 10,000 monthly exemption, with the employee SI 6% and capped job-expense allowances removed first; "other income" at a flat 15% with no exemption; BIK added to the progressive base. Top rate 15%.
* Non-resident: a single flat 20% on gross taxable income with no exemption and no progression — regular salary, other income and valued BIK are all charged at 20%. The residency test is Law Art. 5 (permanent home / ≥183 days / Yemeni with Yemen-source pay).[k][l] Charge scope: non-residents are taxed on Yemen-source income (income realised in / services performed in the Republic; pay from a resident employer even for work abroad is Yemen-source — Arts. 3, 8).
Provide the tax calculation logic covering eligibility, joining month, termination month, mid-month joining, mid-month terminations, and mid-month salary revisions.
* Eligibility: every employee with taxable employment income is within PAYE from the first pay period; exempt categories (Art. 50 — e.g. qualifying daily-wage workers, diplomats under reciprocity) are excluded.
* Joining month / mid-month joining: tax from the first period of employment. For a partial month, pro-rate the exemption and bands by days worked ÷ 30 (Exec Regs Art. 52) for a resident; a non-resident is a flat 20% on the actual pay with no apportionment needed (no exemption to pro-rate).
* Termination month / mid-month termination: tax the final period's pay; pro-rate the resident exemption/bands to the covered days on the 30-day basis. Statutory end-of-service gratuity paid on exit is exempt (Art. 50(d)); any dismissal-before-term or no-notice compensation is taxable.
* Mid-month salary revision: tax follows the pay actually made in the period; for a resident, run the progressive schedule on the month's total taxable salary. Because bands are monthly, a mid-month raise simply increases the month's taxable salary and is taxed under the same 0/10/15% bands (no separate re-rating).
Do tax rates vary by gender or age?
No. Salary-tax rates and the personal exemption do not vary by gender or age. The same schedule applies to all resident individuals and the same flat 20% to all non-residents, regardless of gender or age.
What is the minimum tax rate applicable?
The minimum rate is 0% — the first YER 10,000/month (YER 120,000/year) of a resident's regular salary is exempt. Above the exemption the first taxed band is 10%. For "other income" there is no 0% band (flat 15% from the first rial); for non-residents there is no 0% band (flat 20% from the first rial). So the effective minimum is 0% for a resident's regular salary within the exemption, and the lowest positive salary-band rate is 10%.

6.3 Adjustments and Special Cases
For annualized tax systems, which salary components must be projected?
Yemen operates the salaries tax as a monthly final withholding, not a cumulative annualised (projection) system — so no mandatory forward projection is required. Where an employer chooses to annualise for internal estimation, the components that would be projected are the recurring regular items: basic salary and fixed cash allowances (annualise × 12 against the annual 120k/240k bands and YER 120,000 exemption). Non-recurring "other income" — bonuses, overtime, incentives, session/board allowances, commissions — is not projected into the progressive base; it is taxed as it arises at the flat 15% (resident) or 20% (non-resident). Benefits in kind are valued per Exec Regs Art. 56 for the period in which they arise.
Are year-end tax adjustments required?
No separate year-end reconciliation return is required. The monthly Form 4 is the operative filing, and the year is effectively the sum of the monthly returns; no annual employee-level P60-equivalent was located in the primary sources. If a monthly return is wrong, correct it through the ordinary monthly filing process. Assessment disputes go to a settlement committee (30-day decision) — that is dispute resolution, not a year-end true-up.
Are there minimum or higher tax requirements if employees fail to submit a tax ID or social security number?
The primary sources prescribe no punitive minimum or higher tax rate on the employee for a missing TIN or social-security number. There is no "emergency/default tax rate" mechanism in the located law. What the law does impose is employer-side penalties for non-withholding and late/incorrect filing: failure to withhold/remit is a violation carrying a fine of YER 600,000 (large) / 125,000 (medium) / 5,000 (small) plus the tax, doubled on repeat (Art. 154); late filing attracts 2% of tax due per month or part-month, capped at the tax due (Art. 155). Form 4 requires the employer's TIN and a resident/non-resident headcount split, and enrolment statements carry employee SSNs — so the compliance pressure is on the employer to obtain and report the identifiers, not on applying a higher rate to the employee. Best-supported position: withhold at the correct statutory rate regardless, and remediate the missing identifier; no higher default rate applies (confirm any administrative practice with the Tax Authority).
What is the income tax and social security refund procedure?
No dedicated employee refund form or standing refund mechanism for over-withheld salaries tax was located in the primary sources. Because the tax is a monthly final withholding, over-withholding is normally corrected by adjusting a subsequent monthly Form 4 for the same employer, or resolved through the assessment/objection route to the settlement committee (which must decide within 30 days). For social insurance, no refund procedure is published in the source set; a mis-stated monthly contribution is corrected via the ordinary monthly GCSS schedule. State both as best-supported positions with the caveat that the exact refund/adjustment forms are an administrative gap to confirm with the Tax Authority and GCSS respectively.

Module 7: Deductions, Exemptions & Benefits
This module sets out how Yemeni law treats statutory tax reliefs and exemptions, non-cash (in-kind) benefits, and third-party deductions such as court-ordered garnishments. The rules combine the Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 and its Executive Regulations, the Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 (as amended), and the Social Insurance Law of 1991. All amounts are stated in Yemeni Rial (YER), the mandatory currency for wage payment (Labour Law Art. 61).
7.1 Statutory Tax Reliefs, Deductions, and Exemptions
For each statutory tax relief, deduction, exemption, or other tax advantage, provide the Name and Definition, Eligibility Rules, Statutory Limits & Amounts, Calculation Method, Application Point, and Employee Declaration Requirements
Yemen's salaries-tax regime relies on a small set of clearly defined reliefs rather than a broad menu of allowances. The centrepiece is a single personal exemption of YER 10,000 per month (YER 120,000 per year) that is deducted from a resident's regular salary before the progressive rate schedule is applied (Income Tax Law Art. 36; Exec Regs Art. 52). The other reliefs work by removing specific amounts from taxable salary entirely: the employee's own statutory social-insurance contribution, job-expense allowances within fixed money caps, and a defined list of statutorily exempt receipts such as end-of-service entitlements and work-injury compensation (Income Tax Law Arts. 49-50; Exec Regs Art. 57). Each relief is summarised below, followed by its eligibility, statutory limit, calculation method, the point at which it is applied in the payroll run, and any employee-declaration requirement.
The personal exemption is available to resident individuals only. Non-residents receive no exemption; their salary is taxed at a flat 20% on the gross (Income Tax Law Art. 37; Form 4). The personal exemption is granted once per taxpayer per year even where the taxpayer is liable to more than one direct tax; the taxpayer chooses which tax it is set against (Income Tax Law Art. 51; Exec Regs Art. 52(c)). It is pro-rated for part-periods (for example a starter or leaver) on a 30-day-month basis (Exec Regs Art. 52).

	Are salary sacrifice arrangements allowed?
Salary-sacrifice arrangements are not recognised as a distinct tax-planning mechanism in Yemeni law. The only amounts that receive pre-tax treatment are the statutory social-insurance, pension and health contributions and the capped job-expense allowances listed above (Income Tax Law Art. 49; Exec Regs Art. 57). An employee cannot voluntarily convert taxable cash salary into a tax-free benefit; ordinary private savings and voluntary contributions are not pre-tax, and benefits in kind provided in place of cash are themselves taxable (Exec Regs Art. 56). In practice, any "sacrifice" of cash for a non-cash benefit simply moves the value into the taxable benefit-in-kind regime rather than out of tax.
List common types of post-tax deductions and any specific ordering rules or limitations
Post-tax deductions are amounts taken from net pay after income tax has been calculated. Yemeni law keeps this layer narrow. The recognised categories are: wage attachments (garnishments) made under a final court judgment (Labour Law Art. 63); instalments to recover damage or loss caused by the worker's fault, capped at 25% of the basic wage per month (Labour Law Art. 64, read with disciplinary fines under Art. 99); and any voluntary deductions the employee has agreed to (for example private savings), which are not tax-relieved. The Labour Law does not publish a single ranked priority list, but the practical ordering is (1) statutory social insurance and income tax first, as pre-net obligations; (2) final court-ordered attachments; (3) the damage-recovery instalment within its 25%-of-basic-wage cap; and (4) voluntary deductions last, only to the extent net pay remains. The overriding protection is that a worker's wage claim ranks as a first-priority claim over the employer's assets (Labour Law Art. 8), and no deduction other than a final court judgment may attach the wage without agreement (Art. 63).
List any income types that are statutorily exempt from income tax
The following salary-related receipts are exempt from salaries tax under Income Tax Law Art. 50 and Art. 49: retirement pensions and end-of-service entitlements (Art. 50(d)); work-accident and work-injury compensation, including lifetime and post-death allowances (Art. 50(c)); compensations and medical assistance paid for work burdens under documented medical reports (Art. 50(e)); income of qualifying foreign diplomatic/consular staff on a reciprocity basis (Art. 50(a)); income of non-Yemeni staff of international organisations and aid bodies for official duties (Art. 50(b)); daily-wage workers' income within the Exec-Regs conditions (Art. 50(f)); the employee's statutory social-insurance, pension and health contributions (Art. 49); and job-expense reimbursements within the transport (YER 30,000) and representation-plus-appearance (YER 35,000) caps (Exec Regs Art. 57). Note the sharp distinction between the exempt statutory end-of-service gratuity and taxable compensation for dismissal before end of service or dismissal without notice, which are taxable cash benefits (Exec Regs Art. 55 items 6-7).

7.2 Benefits in Kind (BIK)
Are non-cash benefits taxed via the standard payroll system, or a separate employer-paid regime?
Non-cash benefits are taxed through the standard payroll (salaries-tax) system, not through a separate employer-paid fringe-benefits tax. Yemen has no distinct Fringe Benefits Tax (FBT) regime. Instead, the money value of a taxable benefit in kind is added to the employee's taxable salary and taxed in the employee's own hands at the ordinary salaries-tax rates, with the employer withholding the resulting tax (Income Tax Law Art. 48; Exec Regs Arts. 55-56).
What is the definition of fringe benefits and how is the taxable amount evaluated?
A taxable benefit in kind is any advantage or benefit that the employer provides in a form other than cash and that is not required for the performance of the job (Exec Regs Art. 56). The taxable amount is the assessed money value of the benefit, determined item by item under Exec Regs Art. 56: in-kind housing is valued at the estimated rental value used for the property-rent tax; furnishings are valued by adding furniture depreciation; free electricity and water at cost/value; free phone, internet, fax and mobile handsets; grants and in-kind gifts; laptops and computers except those dedicated to the job; free meals not required by the work; insurance premiums; and in-kind clothing except required uniforms. Benefits that are genuinely required to do the job — for example required uniforms and job-dedicated equipment — are not taxable (Exec Regs Art. 56).
If a separate FBT regime exists, describe it in detail
Not Applicable — Yemen does not operate a separate employer-paid fringe-benefits tax; benefits in kind are taxed to the employee through payroll.
What is the calculation logic for fringe benefit tax?
Not Applicable as a standalone FBT calculation — there is no separate FBT. The operative logic is that the assessed money value of each taxable benefit (per Exec Regs Art. 56) is added to the employee's gross taxable salary, and the combined amount is taxed on the ordinary salaries-tax schedule (0/10/15% for residents after the personal exemption; flat 20% for non-residents). Where the employer also pays the tax on the employee's behalf, that tax is itself a taxable benefit and must be grossed up (Exec Regs Art. 55 item 18).
Are benefits-in-kind also subject to withholding tax?
Yes. Because benefits in kind are treated as part of taxable salary, they are subject to the employer's monthly withholding obligation just like cash salary (Income Tax Law Art. 48; Exec Regs Arts. 54-55). The employer must withhold and remit the tax attributable to the benefit's assessed value through the monthly Form 4 return.
Which benefits-in-kind must be processed via payroll, and which require declarations?
All taxable benefits in kind must be valued and run through payroll so the correct tax is withheld — this covers in-kind housing and furnishings, free utilities, phone/internet, non-job travel tickets, gifts, non-required meals, insurance premiums, non-uniform clothing, and non-job-dedicated computers (Exec Regs Art. 56). Benefits that are exempt or excluded — required uniforms, job-dedicated equipment, and reimbursed job travel/per-diems — are not taxed but should be documented so their exclusion can be justified (Exec Regs Arts. 56-57). Form 4 requires benefits and similar "other income not included in the salary sheet" to be declared on row 2 of the return grid, so the value processed in payroll is also reflected in the monthly declaration.

7.3 Garnishments and Court Orders
What are the rules for processing statutory deductions like child support, court-ordered fines, or tax levies?
Statutory and third-party deductions such as child-support orders, court-ordered fines and tax levies are processed as post-tax deductions from net pay, and Yemeni law makes them the exception rather than the norm. The controlling rule is that wages due to a worker may not be attached (garnished) except under a final court judgment, unless the employer and worker have agreed otherwise (Labour Law Art. 63). This means a payroll administrator should act on a garnishment only where there is a final, enforceable court judgment (or the worker's own agreement); an unadjudicated demand does not authorise a wage deduction. A tax levy for the employee's own unpaid tax would likewise require enforcement through the court/collection process before it can attach the wage. Child support and similar family-maintenance orders are given effect through the same route: a final court judgment.
Is there a statutory priority order if an employee has multiple garnishments?
The Labour Law does not set out an itemised statutory ranking for multiple competing garnishments. What the law does establish is a hierarchy of protection: the worker's wage entitlement itself ranks as a first-priority claim over all the employer's movable and immovable assets, ahead of other creditors (Labour Law Art. 8). Within the deductions that may lawfully be taken from the wage, the practical order is (1) statutory pre-net obligations (social insurance and income tax); (2) attachments made under a final court judgment (Art. 63); and (3) the employer's recovery of damage caused by the worker, which is instalment-limited (Art. 64). Where multiple final court judgments compete, the terms and dates of the judgments themselves govern their ranking; absent a specific statutory tie-break in the Labour Law, the court order determines allocation.
Are there protected earnings limits?
Two protections operate. First, only a final court judgment (or the worker's agreement) can attach the wage at all (Labour Law Art. 63) — this is a gatekeeping protection rather than a percentage cap. Second, for the specific case of the employer recovering damage or loss caused by the worker's fault or negligence, the monthly instalment deducted may not exceed 25% of the basic wage (Labour Law Art. 64, read with Art. 99 on disciplinary fines). The 25% cap is expressly measured against the basic wage (الأجر الأساسي) — pay for the work itself, excluding allowances (Labour Law Art. 2) — not against the full wage or net pay. The Labour Law does not fix a separate percentage cap for court-ordered garnishments generally; the amount is set by the final judgment, subject to the wage-protection principles of the Code.

The source is the Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 (as amended by Laws 25/1997, 11/2001 and 25/2003), read with the Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 for the tax status of leave pay and the Social Insurance Law of 1991 for contribution effects. All amounts are in Yemeni Rial (YER). For applying the Code, a year is 365 days and a month is 30 days unless stated otherwise (Labour Law Art. 9).
8.1 Leave Entitlements
For each type of statutory leave, what are the eligibility criteria and accrual methodology?
Yemeni law provides a defined set of paid statutory leaves — annual, sick, maternity, Hajj, casual and widow bereavement — plus unpaid leave at the employer's discretion. Annual leave accrues steadily across the year; the other leaves are event-driven entitlements triggered by illness, childbirth, pilgrimage, personal emergency or bereavement rather than by monthly accrual. Pay during leave is generally the worker's full wage, except sick leave, which steps down over time. Each leave type is set out below with its eligibility, accrual basis and pay rate.

How are unpaid or partially paid leaves handled?
Partially paid leave arises principally in the sick-leave tiers (85%, 75%, 50%) and where the widow's bereavement leave continues beyond the paid 40 days into the unpaid iddah balance. In these cases the worker is paid the applicable percentage of the wage for the paid portion and nothing for the unpaid portion. Fully unpaid leave (Art. 86) is granted at the employer's discretion; during it the employment relationship continues but no wage is payable. During any paid leave, the worker is prohibited from working for wages elsewhere; if the worker is found to have done so the employer may recover the leave pay, provided this does not terminate service (Art. 88).
Which wage types are affected by unpaid/partially paid leave?
During unpaid leave, all wage-based components stop: basic wage, all cash allowances, and any pay-linked variable elements are not paid for the unpaid days. Because the social-insurance base is basic salary plus allowances, an unpaid period reduces or removes the covered earnings for that period. During partially paid sick leave, the wage components are paid at the reduced tier percentage (for example 85%). Job-expense reimbursements that are tied to actually performing the job (such as transport allowance) would not accrue for days not worked. Overtime and bonuses, which are event- and performance-driven, do not accrue during leave.
Are there any mandatory national military service leaves?
Not Applicable — no compulsory national military-service leave is provided in the Labour Law within payroll scope, and military/security personnel are outside the scope of the Code entirely (Labour Law Art. 3). No statutory paid military-service leave for private-sector employees was located.
Are any other kinds of leave paid by government authorities?
With the exception of work-injury/occupational-disease absence, which is handled under the Social Insurance Law framework (Art. 83; SI Law 1991), statutory leave in Yemen is employer-paid. Maternity, annual, sick (in the employer-paid tiers), Hajj, casual and the paid portion of bereavement leave are all borne by the employer, not reimbursed by a government authority. Work-injury income support and related benefits flow through the social-insurance system (General Corporation for Social Security) for covered workers; the exact reimbursement mechanics sit with the GCSS and are an administrative matter for the specific employer.
What are the payout rules for statutory leaves?
During employment, paid leave is paid at the worker's full wage (or the applicable sick-leave tier), and the worker may not convert annual leave into cash while employed (Art. 79(6)). There is therefore no in-service encashment of annual leave. The one payout scenario the Code recognises is on termination: accrued but untaken leave forms part of the worker's final entitlements and cannot be forfeited (see Art. 120(3) and Module 9).
How are unused paid leaves treated upon termination?
Unused accrued annual leave is paid out on termination as part of the worker's final entitlements; it cannot be waived or forfeited in any case of contract termination (Art. 120(3), read with Art. 79). The Code does not set a distinct leave-encashment formula, so the standard approach is to pay the accrued days at the worker's full wage. The month-30-days / year-365-days convention (Art. 9) is used to convert accrued days into a money amount from the monthly wage. See Module 9.1 for the calculation and tax treatment of leave paid on termination.
Provide the calculation logic for each leave type where applicable
Annual leave accrues at not less than 2.5 days per month of actual service to at least 30 days per year (Art. 79(1)); pay per day is the full wage divided by 30. Sick-leave pay is the applicable tier percentage of the wage for the days in that tier (Art. 80(1)). Maternity pay is 60 days at full wage, extended by 20 days at full wage where a medical report confirms a difficult birth or twins (Art. 45). Hajj, casual and the first 40 days of widow bereavement leave are paid at full wage for the entitlement days. For all daily conversions the month is treated as 30 days (Art. 9): daily wage = monthly full wage ÷ 30.
How does extended unpaid leave impact social security contributions, tax status, and accrual?
During extended unpaid leave, no wage is paid, so there is no taxable salary for the period and no income tax arises on it. Because the social-insurance base is the wage actually paid (basic + allowances), an unpaid period produces no covered earnings and therefore no employee 6% or employer 9% contribution for those days; continuity of the insured record for a long unpaid gap is an administrative matter to confirm with the GCSS, for which no statutory floor/ceiling or continuity rule was located in our sources. Leave and service accrual likewise pause: annual-leave accrual is tied to actual service (Art. 79(1)), so periods of unpaid leave do not build annual-leave entitlement, and only periods of actual service count toward the end-of-service gratuity computation (Art. 120). The employment relationship itself continues during authorised unpaid leave, so the worker's other statutory protections remain in place.

Module 9: Termination & Irregular Payments
This module sets out how Mercans calculates and taxes payments made on termination of employment, and how it handles irregular and retrospective payments such as bonuses and back pay. The rules combine the Labour Law No. 5 of 1995 (as amended) for the entitlements themselves, the Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010 and its Executive Regulations for the tax treatment, and the Social Insurance Law of 1991 for contribution effects. All amounts are in Yemeni Rial (YER). A year is 365 days and a month is 30 days for applying the Labour Law (Art. 9).
9.1 Termination Payments
What are the different types of termination payments?
When employment ends, a worker may be due several distinct payments, each with its own tax treatment. The principal components are: (1) the statutory end-of-service gratuity or the social-insurance pension/lump sum (Labour Law Art. 120); (2) payment for unused accrued annual leave (Art. 120(3), read with Art. 79); (3) notice-period pay, or pay in lieu of notice, equal to the wage-payment period (Art. 38); (4) where the dismissal is arbitrary or unfair, additional compensation set by the Arbitration Committee not exceeding 6 months' wage (Art. 39); and (5) any compensation for dismissal before end of service or dismissal without notice (Exec Regs Art. 55 items 6-7). Ordinary final salary up to the leaving date is also paid, the day after the contract ends (or within 6 days for a voluntary resignation) (Art. 65).

	How do termination rules and calculations differ by residential status and employer type?
The Labour Law entitlements do not vary by residency — a resident and a non-resident private-sector worker are both entitled to the Art. 120 gratuity and the other statutory payments. What differs is the tax treatment of the taxable elements: for a resident, taxable termination pay (unused leave, notice pay, dismissal compensation) is taxed on the progressive resident schedule / as other income at 15%, whereas for a non-resident the taxable elements are taxed at the flat 20% with no exemption (Income Tax Law Art. 37). The exempt elements (end-of-service gratuity, SI pension, work-injury compensation) remain exempt regardless of residency (Art. 50). By employer type, the key split is whether the worker is covered by social insurance: an insured worker takes the SI pension or lump sum under the 1991 law (Art. 120(1)), while an uninsured worker is owed the employer-paid gratuity of at least one month's last wage per year of service (Art. 120(2)). Public-sector employees are outside Labour Law scope (Art. 3) and are not in payroll scope here.
Detail the calculation and tax withholding rules for unused leave paid out on termination
Unused accrued annual leave is paid at the worker's full wage. The day value is the monthly full wage divided by 30 (Labour Law Art. 9), multiplied by the number of accrued unused days. Unlike the end-of-service gratuity, leave paid out on termination is not within the Art. 50 exemptions; it is a payment arising from the employment and is taxable as salary (Income Tax Law Art. 48). Where it is paid together with the final regular salary it is taxed on the ordinary schedule; where paid as a separate lump sum on top of the final salary it is more prudently treated as "other income" at the flat 15% (resident) or 20% (non-resident). Leave-payout is not part of the social-insurance base as a bonus/lump sum is excluded; only regular covered earnings feed the SI base.
Describe the rules for statutory severance/redundancy payments
The core provision is Labour Law Art. 120. On end of service, an insured worker is entitled to a monthly pension or lump-sum gratuity under the Social Insurance Law (or a more favourable special scheme) (Art. 120(1)). A worker who is not covered by social insurance is owed an employer-paid end-of-service gratuity of at least one month's salary for each year of service, computed on the worker's last month's wage (Art. 120(2)). This gratuity is non-forfeitable in every case of termination — it is not reduced or withheld for resignation, for (non-summary) dismissal, or for redundancy (Art. 120(3)). Redundancy proper — termination for a proven technical/economic workforce reduction (Art. 36(c)) — does not remove the Art. 120 entitlement. Where the employer terminates arbitrarily, the worker is additionally entitled to special compensation set by the competent Arbitration Committee, not exceeding 6 months' wage, on top of notice pay and other entitlements (Art. 39).

9.2 Irregular and Retrospective Payments
Describe the specific tax calculation method required for irregular payments
Irregular payments to a salary earner — bonuses, incentives, session or board allowances, commissions, gratuities, grants, fees, overtime, the employee's share of distributed profits, and any sum beyond the full monthly salary and statutory allowances — are taxed at a flat 15% on the gross with no exemption (Income Tax Law Art. 49; Exec Regs Art. 55(b); Form 4 row 2). This is the "other income" stream on the salaries-tax return, distinct from the progressive schedule that applies to regular salary. The personal exemption of YER 10,000 does not apply to these amounts, and no bands are applied — the full 15% is charged on the gross irregular amount. For a non-resident, the flat 20% applies instead (Income Tax Law Art. 37). Critically, these irregular amounts (bonuses and overtime) are excluded from the social-insurance base, so no employee 6% or employer 9% contribution arises on them (SSA SSPTW 2018 base definition; Income Tax Law Art. 49 treatment).
How are backdated payments and retrospective salary adjustments handled?
A retrospective salary increase or back pay is a payment "arising from the work", so it is taxable when paid (Income Tax Law Art. 48). Where the back pay represents additional regular salary for prior periods, it is added to the regular-salary stream; where it is a one-off catch-up beyond the normal monthly salary it takes the character of "other income" and is taxed at the flat 15% (resident) / 20% (non-resident) in the period it is paid. The salaries tax is operated as a monthly final withholding via Form 4 (Income Tax Law Art. 25; Exec Regs Art. 151), and no source in our set prescribes a re-opening of prior monthly returns for a retrospective adjustment; the accepted approach is to bring the arrears into the withholding for the month of payment and declare it on that month's Form 4.
For arrears, how are contributions and deductions computed?
For any element of arrears that forms part of the social-insurance covered earnings (a retrospective increase to basic salary or a covered allowance), the employee 6% and employer 9% contributions are due on the additional covered amount; the contribution is calculated on the extra covered earnings and taken up in the payment period. Arrears that are bonus- or overtime-type "other income" are excluded from the SI base and attract only the flat 15%/20% income tax, not social insurance. Post-tax deductions (final court-ordered garnishments; the damage-recovery instalment capped at 25% of basic wage under Labour Law Art. 64) apply to net pay after these calculations, as in the standard gross-to-net flow.

Module 10: Reporting, Compliance & Finalisation
This module sets out the statutory reporting obligations that Yemen payroll must satisfy, the integrations required, the mandatory content of an employee payslip, and the year-end finalisation process. Field-level layouts for each report are not reproduced here; they are maintained in the Report Specifications.
A structural point applies throughout. Yemen is administratively split between the internationally recognised Government (Aden) and the Sana'a-based de facto authority, but both administrations apply the same pre-war statutory framework: Income Tax Law No. 17 of 2010, the Social Insurance Law of 1991, and Labour Law No. 5 of 1995. The reporting obligations below therefore hold in both zones. The divergence is administrative — two tax administrations, two social-security administrations, two central banks, and two exchange rates — rather than a difference of statute. Amounts are always reported in Yemeni Rial (YER).
10.1 Statutory Reporting
Describe the country's primary digital payroll reporting system
Yemen does not operate a single unified real-time digital payroll platform of the kind seen in more developed e-government jurisdictions. The primary statutory payroll filing is the Salaries and Wages Tax Return, Form No. 4 (ضريبة المرتبات والأجور), submitted to the Tax Authority under the Ministry of Finance. The Tax Authority publishes Law 17/2010 and its forms on its portal, and larger taxpayers increasingly file and pay through Tax Authority branches, the Central Bank, or authorised banks; however, Form 4 remains fundamentally a structured declaration (paper template with a portal/branch submission channel) rather than a payslip-level electronic feed. The second statutory reporting stream is the monthly social-insurance contribution return to the General Corporation for Social Security (GCSS). Because collection is administered separately in Aden and Sana'a, the operative submission channel depends on the zone in which the employer is registered.

10.2 Integrations
Is there any mandatory inbound integration?
Not Applicable — no mandatory inbound integration to the payroll system is verified in the source set. Yemen has no government-operated data feed (such as a tax-office rate/threshold push or a benefits-agency inbound file) that payroll is statutorily required to consume. Master-data inputs (TIN, social-security registration) are obtained through registration processes, not through an automated statutory inbound integration.
Is there any mandatory outbound integration beyond standard reporting?
Not Applicable — no mandatory outbound integration exists beyond the standard statutory returns themselves. The outbound obligations are the Form 4 salaries-tax filing (with payment to the Tax Authority treasury, an accepted cheque, or a direct bank remittance) and the monthly GCSS contribution return. There is no separately mandated real-time reporting stream, e-invoicing-style payroll feed, or bank-directed payroll clearing integration required by statute. Salary payment itself must be made in Yemeni currency at the workplace on the statutory schedule (Labour Law Art. 61); bank disbursement is a commercial arrangement, not a mandated statutory integration.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Art. 25; Form No. 4; Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 61.

10.4 Other Compliance
Is payroll software certification mandatory?
Not Applicable — no payroll-software certification requirement is located in the source set. Neither Law 17/2010 and its Executive Regulations nor the Labour Law imposes a fiscalisation or certified-software obligation on payroll systems. The statutory duties fall on the employer as taxpayer (correct withholding, timely filing and remittance, retention and production of books and records on demand), not on an accredited software product. Employers must keep proper books and records and produce salary/wage/bonus data on request so the Authority can verify the salaries tax paid; failure to keep proper books is separately penalised (lump-sum fine, evasion chapter). This is a record-keeping obligation, not a software-certification regime. Note also that Yemen has no comprehensive data-protection statute of the GDPR type; record retention is governed by the tax-law bookkeeping provisions.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 (bookkeeping and information provisions); Executive Regulations Art. 151.
10.5 Year-End Processing and Finalisation
Describe the mandatory year-end finalisation process
The Yemeni salaries tax is operated as a final monthly withholding through the monthly Form 4 return. No separate annual employee-level salaries reconciliation return (the equivalent of a P60 or an annual withholding reconciliation) is located in Law 17/2010 or its Executive Regulations. The best-supported position is therefore that year-end is effectively the sum of the twelve monthly Form 4 returns (or four quarterly returns for small/micro enterprises): the operative filing is the monthly return, and there is no additional annual salaries-tax filing to close the year. Caveat: if a distinct annual salaries reconciliation form exists in current administrative practice, it is not evident in the primary statutes and should be confirmed against a clean copy of the Executive Regulations and with the Tax Authority branch. The tax year is the calendar year, 1 January to 31 December (Art. 7).
Practically, finalisation consists of: confirming that all twelve monthly Form 4 returns (and GCSS returns) have been filed and paid within their deadlines; reconciling the year's withheld tax and remittances against the payroll ledger; ensuring the foreign-labour, new-employee and terminated-employee statements were attached each month; and retaining the books and records for production on demand.
What documentation or annual statements must be provided to employees?
There is no statutory annual tax certificate that the employer must issue to each employee (no P60-equivalent is mandated). The mandatory employee-facing document is the per-period payslip under Labour Law Art. 66 (see 10.3). On termination, the worker is entitled to settlement of final entitlements. Where an employee needs a record of tax withheld — for example to support a personal filing or a tax-clearance requirement — the employer draws it from the monthly Form 4 records, but issuing an annual employee statement is a matter of practice rather than a statutory obligation.
Is there a formal process for correcting errors discovered after the fiscal year has been closed?
Because there is no formal annual reconciliation return that "locks" the year, a correction is made by revisiting the affected monthly return(s). The statutory framework anticipates post-filing adjustment through the assessment and objection machinery: understated or incomplete returns attract tiered penalties (50% rising to 100% of the tax on the omitted amount, Art. 157), and disputes over an assessment go to a settlement committee that must decide within 30 days (Executive Regulations). The best-supported position is: (1) an employer that discovers an under-withholding after year-end should file/amend and remit the additional tax, expecting a late-payment penalty of 2% of the tax due per month or part-month, capped at the tax due (Art. 155(a)); (2) an over-withholding is resolved as an assessment adjustment through the Tax Authority rather than by unilateral offset. Caveat: the Executive Regulations do not spell out a self-service amended-return mechanism for salaries tax, so the correction route in practice runs through the Tax Authority branch and the settlement committee.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Arts. 7, 25, 154, 155, 157; Executive Regulations Art. 151 (annual reconciliation not located — best-supported position stated); Labour Law 5/1995 Art. 66.

Module 11: Advanced Scenarios & Cross-Cutting Logic
This module covers the calculation logic that applies across scenarios — proration, projection, mid-period rate changes, multiple payments in a period, and pay-cycle changes — and the treatment of special employee groups and income types. All amounts are in Yemeni Rial (YER). Where the statutes are silent, the best-supported position is stated with a caveat rather than left open.
11.1 Calculation Logic
How is the proration and projection logic applied for income tax, social security, exemptions, and deductions?
Yemen operates the salaries tax as a monthly withholding, so proration is fundamentally period-based rather than an annualise-then-divide projection. The Executive Regulations fix the apportionment convention: a month is treated as 30 days and a year as 365 days (Executive Regulations Art. 52; Labour Law Art. 9). For a partial month — a mid-month hire, a final-settlement month, or a mid-month status change — the personal exemption is pro-rated on a days-over-30 basis. The monthly personal exemption of YER 10,000 becomes (days worked ÷ 30) × YER 10,000, and the progressive monthly bands (0–10,000 at 0%; 10,000–20,000 at 10%; above 20,000 at 15%) apply to the pro-rated month. Social insurance is charged on the covered earnings actually paid in the period (basic + allowances, excluding bonuses and overtime) at employee 6% / employer 9%, so it prorates naturally with the earnings paid — there is no separate SI proration formula and no published ceiling or floor to cap it (compute on full covered earnings; confirm any ceiling with the GCSS for a specific employer). Pre-tax deductions (the statutory SI/pension/health contribution) reduce taxable income in the period they are withheld. Job-expense allowance caps (transport ≤ YER 30,000; representation + appearance ≤ YER 35,000) are monthly caps and are pro-rated on the same 30-day basis for a partial month. Because the withholding is periodic and the top marginal rate caps at 15% for residents, there is no year-end true-up projection built into the statute; each month stands on its own. Caveat: the salaries-tax provisions prescribe no explicit monetary rounding rule — standard practice is to compute in whole rials.
If tax or contribution rates change mid-pay period, which rates apply?
No 2026 change to the salaries-tax schedule, the exemption, or the SI rates has occurred, so this is a contingency rule rather than a live event. The best-supported position, given the monthly-withholding design and the 30-day apportionment convention, is that a statutory change takes effect from the pay period specified in the amending decree; within a single monthly period the rate in force on the pay date governs the whole period, because the salaries tax is assessed on the period as a unit and there is no statutory rule splitting a month across two rate regimes. If an amending decree itself specifies a split-period apportionment, that decree controls. Caveat: the statutes do not contain an explicit mid-period changeover rule for salaries tax, so confirm the effective-date treatment in the text of any future amendment.
How are tax and social security handled when an employee is paid multiple times in a single pay period?
Where an employee receives more than one payment in the same monthly period, the tax is not applied independently to each payment as if each were a full month. The regular salary components are aggregated into the Row 1 base for the period and taxed once through the progressive bands after a single monthly exemption (only one exemption applies per period — Art. 51 / Executive Regulations Art. 52(c) confirm a single exemption even across multiple charges). "Other income" payments (a bonus, an incentive, a commission paid in the same month) are aggregated into the Row 2 base and taxed at the flat 15% with no exemption, separately from the salary. Social insurance is charged on the aggregate covered earnings for the period (excluding the bonus/overtime elements). The practical rule for an off-cycle or supplementary run in the same month is therefore: recompute the period on a cumulative basis so the exemption and the bands are applied only once, then withhold the incremental tax.
How is payroll calculated if an employee's pay cycle changes mid-year?
If an employee's pay cycle changes during the year — for example from half-monthly to monthly, or the reverse — each period is assessed on its own terms using the 30-day-month convention, and the exemption is granted per the period covered. A move to monthly pay means one YER 10,000 monthly exemption per month; a half-monthly cycle means the exemption is apportioned to each half-month so that the full YER 10,000 is granted across the month, not doubled. The controlling principle is that the annual exemption is a single YER 120,000 (YER 10,000 × 12) regardless of pay frequency, so on a cycle change the system must ensure the cumulative exemption granted for the year does not exceed the days actually covered × (YER 10,000 ÷ 30). Wage-payment frequency itself follows Labour Law Art. 61 (monthly no later than the 6th of the following month; half-monthly by the 3rd; hourly/daily/weekly at least weekly). Caveat: the statutes prescribe the exemption and the 30-day convention but not an explicit cycle-change algorithm, so the cumulative-exemption approach is the best-supported reading.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Arts. 36, 48, 49, 51; Executive Regulations Arts. 52, 55, 57; Labour Law 5/1995 Arts. 9, 61; Social Insurance Law 1991.
11.2 Special Employee Groups & Income
How does taxation and social security differ for part-time employees and Board of Directors?
Part-time employees are taxed under the ordinary rules: their regular pay runs through Row 1 (resident progressive 0/10/15% after the monthly exemption; non-resident flat 20%), and social insurance applies to their covered earnings at 6% / 9%. A part-time worker who is genuinely casual/daily-wage may fall under the daily-wage exemption (see below), but a regular part-timer with a fixed periodic wage is treated as an ordinary employee. Board of Directors income is expressly within the salaries-tax charge: salaries, bonuses and allowances to chairmen and members of boards of public- and mixed-sector units, and to chairmen, members and managers of capital companies, are taxable (Law Art. 48(c)–(d)). Board session/sitting allowances and board bonuses are "other income" taxed at the flat 15% with no exemption (Art. 49 / Executive Regulations Art. 55(b)), and they are excluded from the SI base. A director who is also a regular salaried employee has the salary taxed progressively (Row 1) and the board fees/allowances taxed at flat 15% (Row 2).
How is taxation handled for employees with multiple employers?
Each employer withholds salaries tax on the income it pays, under the monthly Form 4 mechanism. The statute allows only one personal exemption across all charges — a taxpayer subject to several direct taxes still receives a single YER 120,000/year (YER 10,000/month) exemption and chooses which charge it is applied against (Art. 51 / Executive Regulations Art. 52(c)). The practical consequence is that the exemption should be claimed against only one employer's payroll; if more than one employer applies the full exemption, the taxpayer has under-withheld and the position is resolved through the Tax Authority. There is no employer-to-employer aggregation mechanism in the payroll itself; each employer withholds on its own payments and the single-exemption reconciliation is a taxpayer-level matter. Caveat: the statutes do not describe an automated multi-employer reconciliation, so in practice the exemption is administered against the primary employer.
What is the treatment of foreign-sourced employment income for residents?
The salaries-tax source rules pull in more than purely domestic pay. Income from services performed in the Republic and income paid by a resident employer even where the work is done abroad are Yemen-source (Art. 3). A resident natural person is taxed on income realised inside the Republic; and a Yemeni who works abroad but receives his employment income from the Republic is treated as resident and within the charge (Art. 5(3), Art. 8). Conversely, employment income of a resident that is genuinely foreign-sourced (foreign employer, work performed abroad, paid from abroad) is outside the Yemeni salaries-tax charge for a natural person, because resident natural persons are taxed on income realised inside the Republic rather than on worldwide employment income. The determining factors are the source of the pay (Yemeni employer / Yemen treasury) and the place the work is performed. Caveat: the interaction of Art. 3, Art. 5 and Art. 8 is source-driven; a specific cross-border case should be tested against those three articles together.
How do employer stock option plans work and how are they taxed?
Not directly addressed as a named category in Law 17/2010 or its Executive Regulations. The best-supported position is that a share-based benefit provided to an employee is a taxable benefit under the broad charging rule: the salaries tax applies to all amounts and advantages, cash or in-kind, arising from the employment (Art. 48), and the Executive Regulations tax in-kind benefits, grants and gifts valued per Art. 56. A share award or the discount/gain on an option would therefore be a taxable in-kind employment benefit, valued at its money value when it accrues to the employee, and taxed as employment income (progressively for a resident, flat 20% for a non-resident). It is not part of the social-insurance covered-earnings base (which is cash covered earnings excluding bonuses). Caveat: there is no specific stock-option valuation or timing rule (grant vs vest vs exercise) in the statutes — value the benefit when it is realised as employment income and treat it as an Art. 56 in-kind benefit; confirm valuation with the Tax Authority for a material plan.
How is payroll handled for employees who transfer between different legal entities mid-year?
A transfer between two separate legal entities is treated as a cessation with one employer and a commencement with another, each entity being a distinct taxpayer filing its own Form 4. Each entity withholds on the income it pays; the personal exemption is apportioned to the days each entity employed the individual (days ÷ 30 × YER 10,000 per month), so the full-year exemption is not duplicated across the two entities. Statutory end-of-service and accrued-leave entitlements crystallise per employer under Labour Law Art. 120 unless service is formally carried over by agreement. The SI record follows the employee via the GCSS registration. Caveat: where the transfer is within a group and service continuity is preserved by contract, the two entities should coordinate so the combined exemption granted matches the days worked.
What is the process for an employee who changes citizenship or residency status during the fiscal year?
Residency is tested under Art. 5 (permanent home in Yemen, or ≥ 183 days in a 12-month window, or a Yemeni receiving Yemen-source employment income). Where an employee's residency status changes mid-year, the withholding basis changes prospectively from the period in which the new status applies: periods as a resident are taxed progressively with the monthly exemption; periods as a non-resident are taxed at the flat 20% with no exemption. Because the 183-day test looks at a 12-month window, a status determination can be retrospective — a worker who crosses the 183-day threshold may be reclassified as resident, and any earlier over-withholding at the non-resident 20% rate is resolved as an assessment adjustment through the Tax Authority rather than by unilateral payroll offset. A change of citizenship matters chiefly through its effect on the Art. 5 tests (notably the "Yemeni receiving Yemen-source pay" limb). Caveat: the statutes give the residency tests but not a step-by-step mid-year re-characterisation procedure — apply the new status prospectively and reconcile prior periods through the Authority.
Are there specific rules for expatriates or inpatriates?
There are specific rules. A non-resident (typically an expatriate on short assignment) is taxed at a flat 20% on salaries with no exemption (Art. 37 / Executive Regulations Art. 55(c) / Form 4). Resident foreign employees are taxed on the same progressive schedule as Yemeni residents. Certain expatriate categories are exempt subject to conditions: income of foreign diplomatic and consular staff for their official functions, subject to reciprocity and a Foreign-Ministry attestation (Art. 50(a)); and income of non-Yemeni experts at international organisations under public international law, or non-Yemeni staff of bodies operating under aid agreements with the Yemeni government for aid services to Yemen (Art. 50(b)) — this does not cover ordinary jobs at UN/aid-agency offices. Note the administrative linkage: for foreign staff, residence-renewal and exit can be tied to a tax-clearance/exemption certificate, so payroll should ensure the withholding position is documented. The daily-wage exemption (Art. 50(f)) expressly does not extend to foreign labour or non-residents. Foreign labour must also be reported on the Form 4 foreign-labour statement.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Arts. 3, 5, 8, 37, 48, 49, 50, 51; Executive Regulations Arts. 52, 55, 56, 57; Form No. 4; Labour Law 5/1995 Arts. 61, 120; Social Insurance Law 1991.

Module 12: Gross-to-Net Worked Examples
All figures are in Yemeni Rial (YER) and reuse the canonical worked-example inputs defined in the Facts Base so that every module stays internally consistent. The gross-to-net order is fixed: (1) sum gross earnings; (2) compute social insurance on covered earnings — employee 6% deducted, employer 9% as a parallel on-cost; (3) determine taxable income by removing the employee SI contribution, job-expense allowances within caps, and exempt items; (4) split the taxable amount into regular salary (resident progressive 0/10/15% after the YER 10,000 monthly exemption) and "other income" (flat 15%, no exemption); (5) apply post-tax deductions; (6) net = gross cash − employee SI − income tax − post-tax deductions. The monthly resident bands are 0% on 0–10,000; 10% on 10,000–20,000; 15% above 20,000. Amounts are computed in whole rials (no explicit statutory rounding rule; whole-rial is standard practice).

Module 13: Common Business Practices & Non-Statutory Rules
This module records common business practices that are not fixed by statute but shape how Yemeni payroll is run in practice. These are conventions, not legal requirements; where a practice touches a statutory rule, that rule is cited so the boundary between practice and law is clear. All amounts are in Yemeni Rial (YER).
Off-cycle or ad-hoc payments
There is no statute that authorises or prohibits an off-cycle payroll run; the Labour Law fixes only the outer payment deadlines (monthly wages no later than the 6th of the following month; half-monthly by the 3rd; hourly/daily/weekly at least weekly — Art. 61), and final settlement the day after contract end (Art. 65). Within those limits, off-cycle payments are a matter of employer practice.
Common practice is to use an off-cycle or supplementary run for a defined set of situations: a final settlement on termination (wages, accrued leave, and end-of-service gratuity where the worker is outside social insurance); a correction of a prior under- or over-payment; a bonus or incentive paid outside the normal salary date; and an advance against salary. The recurring compliance point is that an off-cycle payment in the same monthly period must be integrated with the regular run for tax purposes rather than taxed as a fresh full month — the personal exemption and the progressive bands are applied only once per period (see Module 11.1), and "other income" (a bonus, an incentive) is taxed at the flat 15% with no exemption. Social insurance is charged only on the covered-earnings elements of the off-cycle payment (bonuses and overtime are excluded from the SI base). Employers commonly issue a supplementary payslip for the off-cycle amount, since the Art. 66 payslip obligation applies to every payment, not only to the main run.
Legal basis: Labour Law 5/1995 Arts. 61, 65, 66; Income Tax Law 17/2010 Arts. 48, 49 (integration of same-period payments); Social Insurance Law 1991.
Non-recoverable draws against future commissions
Yemeni statute does not regulate commission draws, so the treatment is a matter of contract and practice. A "draw" is an advance paid to a commission earner and later set against commissions earned; a non-recoverable draw is one the employer does not claw back if commissions fall short.
Common practice is to treat a non-recoverable draw as ordinary taxable employment income in the period it is paid, because it is an amount arising from the employment that the employee is not obliged to repay — it falls within the broad salaries-tax charge (Law 17/2010 Art. 48). Where the draw functions as guaranteed regular pay, employers typically run it through the progressive salary schedule (Row 1); where it is structured as an incentive/commission-type payment, it is treated as "other income" at the flat 15% (Row 2). The subsequent commission, when earned, is "other income" taxed at the flat 15%, and the draw already paid is reconciled against it. A recoverable draw (a genuine repayable advance) is, by contrast, not income when advanced — it is a loan recovered from later pay, subject to the general wage-deduction protections (a court order is needed to attach wages under Art. 63, and damage-recovery instalments are capped at 25% of the basic wage under Art. 64, though an agreed advance recovery is a contractual matter). The practical rule: tax a non-recoverable draw when paid; do not double-tax the commission it is later set against. Caveat: because the statutes are silent on draws, the classification (salary vs other income) should follow the substance of the arrangement in the employment contract.
Legal basis: Income Tax Law 17/2010 Arts. 48, 49 (broad charge; other-income flat rate); Labour Law 5/1995 Arts. 63, 64 (wage-deduction limits) — no specific draw statute; treatment is contractual practice.
Common non-statutory leave types
The Labour Law provides a defined set of statutory leave — annual (≥ 30 days), sick (graduated pay over 8 months), maternity (60 days, +20 for difficult birth or twins), Hajj (20 days once after 4 years' service), casual/emergency (up to 10 days per year), unpaid leave at the employer's discretion, and widow bereavement (40 days paid). It does not provide paternity, marriage, or study/exam leave.
In practice, employers commonly grant additional non-statutory leave to fill those gaps and to remain competitive: short paternity leave for a new father (a few days); marriage leave for the employee's own wedding; study or examination leave for employees in accredited programmes; compassionate/bereavement leave for relatives other than the statutory widow case; and discretionary religious or personal leave beyond the casual-leave allowance. There is no statutory template for these, so they are managed under the employer's internal policy or the employment contract, and, where they are not separately provided, they are typically absorbed into the statutory casual-leave allowance (Art. 85, up to 10 days paid per year) or granted as unpaid leave at the employer's discretion (Art. 86).
Reporting of non-statutory leave is an internal-policy and HR matter rather than a statutory filing. It has a payroll consequence only where it affects pay: paid non-statutory leave is part of the wage and is taxed and social-insured as normal in the period; unpaid non-statutory leave reduces the period's earnings and therefore the tax and SI computed on them (with the personal exemption and allowance caps pro-rated on the 30-day-month convention where the leave spans a partial month). None of these non-statutory leave types appears on a statutory return; they are tracked in the HRMS/leave system and reflected in payroll only through their pay impact. Caveat: because these leaves are not statutory, their existence, duration, and paid/unpaid status vary by employer and should be governed by a written policy.
Legal basis: Labour Law 5/1995 Arts. 79–87 (statutory leave; Arts. 85–86 the fallback for non-statutory absences); Art. 9 (30-day month for apportionment) — non-statutory leave types themselves have no statutory basis and are governed by employer policy.
