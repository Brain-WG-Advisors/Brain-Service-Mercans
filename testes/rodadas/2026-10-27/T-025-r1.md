## Resposta

Now the EI page for the equivalent rules.

**Short answer:** CRA does not spread the annual maximum evenly across pay periods. Each period you calculate CPP, CPP2 and EI on that period's actual earnings, check the employee's year-to-date total with your company, and keep deducting until that total reaches the annual maximum. In the period where the maximum is reached, you deduct only the remaining balance. After that, the deduction is zero until January 1.

**2026 annual limits**

| | Ceiling | Rate (employee) | Annual max (employee) |
|---|---|---|---|
| CPP (CPP1) | YMPE $74,600, minus $3,500 basic exemption | 5.95% | $4,230.45 |
| CPP2 | Earnings between $74,600 and $85,000 (YAMPE) | 4% | $416.00 |
| EI (outside Quebec) | Maximum insurable earnings $68,900 | 1.63% | $1,123.07 |

Sources: catax.tools 2026 summary. The CPP2 figures are confirmed in CRA's official table: for 2026, the additional maximum pensionable earnings are $85,000, the rate is 4%, and the maximum employee and employer contribution is $416.

**How each one works per pay period**

*CPP1.* Subtract the per-period share of the basic exemption before applying the rate. If the employee is likely to earn more than the annual exemption, you must subtract the portion of the exemption that applies to that pay period before calculating the period's contribution. For monthly pay, that portion is $291.66. The cap rule comes from CRA's CPP page, under the heading "When to stop deducting CPP contributions": for current employees, stop deducting CPP when the employee reaches the maximum contribution for the year in their employment with you.

*CPP2.* CPP2 has no exemption. It applies only to pensionable earnings above the YMPE, so in practice it starts in the pay period where year-to-date earnings pass $74,600. It stops once the $416 maximum is reached.

*EI.* EI has no exemption either. From CRA's EI page, under "When to deduct EI premiums": premiums are deducted from the first dollar earned up to the yearly maximum, with no age limit. Under "When to stop deducting EI premiums": stop when the employee's annual insurable earnings reach the maximum insurable earnings or the maximum employee premium for the year in their employment with you.

**Rules that commonly break payroll setups**

1. **Maximums apply per employer, not per person.** If an employee joins you mid-year from another employer, you must deduct CPP without regard to what the previous employer took, even if the employee already reached the maximum there. Any overpayment is refunded to the employee when they file their tax return, and the employer is not refunded its share. EI follows the same rule. "Employer" here means a separate business number.
2. **Successor employers are the exception.** After an employer restructuring, the successor employer may, in certain situations, treat the former employer's CPP deductions for the year as its own.
3. **The annual maximum is prorated only in specific events.** CRA lists five: the employee turns 18 in the year, turns 70 in the year, files Form CPT30 during the year, is considered disabled under the CPP, or dies in the year.
4. **The employer share stops at the same time.** The employer matches CPP and CPP2 dollar for dollar and pays 1.4 times the employee's EI premium. If a required deduction is missed, the employer is still deemed to have made it.
5. **Quebec works differently.** If the province of employment is Quebec, you deduct a reduced EI premium plus QPIP premiums, and QPP replaces CPP.

**On the payslip:** show each deduction for the current period alongside its year-to-date total, separately for CPP1, CPP2 and EI. The year-to-date figure is what tells you when to stop and how much to take in the final period.

Legislative references, as listed on the CRA pages: CPP Act s. 8 (employee contributions), s. 9 (employer contributions), s. 19 (basic exemption) and s. 21(1) (amount to be deducted and remitted by the employer). EI Act s. 82(1) (deduction of premiums) and s. 82(2) (maximum deduction by a particular employer).
