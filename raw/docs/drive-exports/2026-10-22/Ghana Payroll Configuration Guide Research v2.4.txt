Country Configuration Guide: Ghana (Fiscal Year 2025)




Module 1: Foundational Jurisdictional Parameters




Purpose: To establish the core environmental settings that govern all payroll operations.


This module defines the fundamental parameters for payroll configuration in Ghana. These settings, including the fiscal year, currency, language, and pay cycle rules, form the bedrock of the system. An accurate configuration in this module is paramount, as any errors will have a cascading and compounding impact on all subsequent calculations, statutory reporting, and overall compliance.


1.1 Fiscal, Currency, and Language


What is the statutory tax year?
The statutory tax year in Ghana is the calendar year, running from 1 January to 31 December.1 This period is mandatory for the assessment of personal income tax for all individuals.
Can employers adopt an alternate tax year? If yes, under what circumstances and what are the implications?
For the purposes of individual income tax withholding (Pay-As-You-Earn, or PAYE), an alternate tax year is not permitted.2 All employee tax calculations must be based on the 1 January to 31 December calendar year.
While corporate entities may adopt a different financial period for their own accounting and corporate income tax filings, the basis period for calculating their employees' income tax liability remains the calendar year in which the income was earned.3 The payroll system must be configured to use the calendar year for all cumulative calculations, tax bracket applications, and annual reporting for employees, irrespective of the employer's corporate fiscal year.
What is the official currency for payroll processing and reporting? Provide the ISO code.
The official currency for all payroll processing, statutory remittances, and reporting is the Ghanaian Cedi. The corresponding ISO 4217 code is GHS.5 All monetary values within the payroll system, including earnings, deductions, and final net pay, must be calculated and stored in GHS.
What is the official language for statutory reports and employee payslips?
The official language for all business, government, and legal communication in Ghana is English.8 Therefore, all statutory reports submitted to the Ghana Revenue Authority (GRA) and the Social Security and National Insurance Trust (SSNIT), as well as employee payslips, must be generated in English.


1.2 Pay Cycles and Dates


What are the permissible payroll payment frequencies in the country?
The most prevalent pay cycle in Ghana is monthly.10 However, the law also permits other frequencies, including weekly, bi-weekly, and fortnightly.11 The payroll system must be capable of processing payroll and applying statutory calculations correctly for any of these frequencies.
For each frequency, detail the corresponding statutory reporting and payment deadlines for both income tax withholding and social security contributions.
Statutory reporting and payment deadlines in Ghana are defined on a monthly basis, regardless of the pay frequency. If an employer runs a weekly payroll, the total taxes and social security contributions withheld from all pay runs within a calendar month must be consolidated and remitted by the following month's deadline.

Describe the statutory rule for handling pay dates that fall on a weekend or public holiday.
Ghana's Labour Act, 2003 (Act 651) requires that wages be paid on a working day.17 While the Act does not explicitly state the procedure for a scheduled pay date falling on a non-working day, the established and compliant business practice is to advance the payment to the last working day immediately preceding the weekend or public holiday.19 The payroll system's calendar and date logic must be configured to automatically apply this "pay before" rule.
What are the common payroll pay dates and cut-off dates for payroll processing?
For monthly payrolls, the most common pay date is the last working day of the month.10 To facilitate this, payroll processing cut-off dates for data collection (e.g., timesheets, new hires) are typically set between the 15th and 20th of the month. This provides adequate time for data validation, payroll processing, review, and bank file generation.


1.3 Extra Pay Period Handling


Describe the statutory handling and calculation adjustments required for fiscal years containing extra pay periods (e.g., 53 weekly pays).
The Ghanaian tax system is fundamentally an annual system, with tax liability determined based on the total income earned within the calendar year.2 There are no specific statutory provisions or special calculation adjustments for handling extra pay periods, such as a 53rd week in a fiscal year.
The income from the extra pay period is simply aggregated with all other income earned during the year. The tax calculation for that period should be based on the cumulative year-to-date earnings, applying the standard annual tax brackets. The lack of a special rule is not an omission but a reflection of the system's design, which prioritizes the total annual income as the basis for final tax liability. Consequently, no complex "smoothing" or alternative calculation methods should be configured in the payroll system, as this would be non-statutory.
Detail the impact on annualized tax calculations and social security contributions.
* Impact on Tax Calculations: The inclusion of an extra pay period's earnings will increase the employee's total annual income. This may push the employee into a higher marginal tax bracket for the year, resulting in a higher overall tax liability and effective tax rate. This is a natural consequence of the progressive tax system and does not require any special system adjustment.
* Impact on Social Security Contributions: Social security contributions are calculated on a per-pay-period basis, subject to the monthly minimum and maximum insurable earnings thresholds.20 The extra pay period is treated as a standard pay period. Contributions are calculated on the basic salary earned in that period, capped at the monthly ceiling, and remitted. The annual insurable earnings are effectively capped at 12 times the prevailing monthly maximum.
If optional adjustment methods are permitted, provide a step-by-step guide.
Not Applicable. Ghanaian tax law does not permit optional adjustment methods for extra pay periods.

1.5 Rounding and Precision Rules


What are the statutory or common business practice rules for rounding final net pay and individual calculation components (tax, social security)? Is rounding (e.g., nearest cent, round down) applied at each step of the calculation or only on the final amounts?
There are no explicit statutory regulations governing rounding rules for payroll calculations. The established and universally accepted business practice is to perform all monetary calculations to two decimal places, reflecting the currency's minor unit, the Pesewa (Gp).6
Rounding to the nearest Pesewa (two decimal places) should be applied at the final stage of each distinct calculation component. For example, the total employee SSNIT contribution is calculated and then rounded to two decimal places. Similarly, the total PAYE tax is calculated and rounded. The final net pay is the result of summing gross earnings and subtracting these individually rounded deduction amounts. Intermediate calculation steps should maintain a higher level of precision to avoid compounding errors.
What level of decimal precision is required for statutory reporting?
All statutory reports and remittance files submitted to the GRA and SSNIT must report monetary values with a precision of two decimal places.

3. Social Security and National Insurance Trust (SSNIT): To register the establishment and its employees for the mandatory national pension scheme.16

* Non-Resident: An individual who does not meet the 183-day threshold. Non-residents are taxed only on their Ghana-sourced income at a flat rate.35
The payroll system must have a field to designate an employee's residency status, as this is a key driver for selecting the correct tax schedule.

What previous employment information must new joiners declare?
There is no statutory requirement for a new employee to declare their cumulative earnings or tax paid from a previous employer within the same tax year for the purpose of ongoing monthly PAYE calculations. However, the employee is ultimately responsible for declaring all sources of income when filing their annual personal income tax return by April 30 of the following year.10 While not a system requirement for monthly processing, collecting an employment history is a standard HR practice and is necessary for expatriates seeking work permits.41


2.3 Data Management and Retention


What are the statutory requirements for payroll record retention (e.g., number of years)?
There are two key statutory retention periods that employers must adhere to:
   1. Tax Records: The Revenue Administration Act, 2016 (Act 915) requires that all records supporting tax filings, including payroll registers, payslips, and proof of payment, be retained for a minimum period of six years from the end of the relevant year.1
   2. Pension Records: The National Pensions Regulations require that records related to pension contributions, such as remittance statements, be kept for at least seven years.44
To ensure compliance with both authorities, the system's data retention and archival policy must be configured for the longer of the two periods, which is seven years.
Are there specific data privacy laws (like GDPR) that govern the storage and handling of employee payroll data?
Yes, the handling of employee personal and payroll data is governed by the Data Protection Act, 2012 (Act 843).45 This Act establishes a legal framework for the protection of personal data and outlines eight core principles, including lawfulness of processing, specification of purpose, data security safeguards, and data subject participation. The payroll system and its operators must ensure robust technical and organizational security measures are in place to prevent unauthorized access, disclosure, or loss of employee data, in compliance with Act 843.
How should historical payroll data (e.g., from a previous provider) be handled for cumulative calculations?
When migrating to a new payroll system mid-year, it is best practice to import the full year-to-date (YTD) payroll data from the previous provider. While monthly PAYE is calculated on a non-cumulative basis per employment, the YTD data is essential for several key functions:
   * Annual Reporting: The employer must submit an "Employer's Annual Tax Deduction Schedule" to the GRA, which requires a full-year summary of earnings and taxes for each employee.40
   * Concessional Tax Calculations: The tax treatment of bonuses is based on a threshold of 15% of the annual basic salary.36 Without YTD data, this threshold cannot be calculated correctly.
   * Employee Annual Statements: The employer must provide employees with a summary of their total annual earnings and deductions.
   * Auditing and Reconciliation: Complete historical data is necessary for internal and external audits.
Therefore, a full YTD data migration, including gross earnings, taxable income, tax paid, and sociable salary, is required for a compliant and functional system implementation.


Module 3: The Gross-to-Net Calculation Framework




Purpose: To establish the high-level, sequential logic of the payroll calculation.


This section provides the definitive, step-by-step model for calculating an employee's net pay from their gross earnings in Ghana. This framework serves as the master blueprint for the payroll system's calculation engine, ensuring that all components are processed in the correct statutory sequence.


3.1 Gross-to-Net Flow


Deliverable: Provide a definitive, step-by-step description or text-based flowchart illustrating the standard Gross-to-Net (G2N) calculation framework. The model must clearly describe the sequential process: 1. Summation of Gross Earnings. 2. Application of Pre-Tax Deductions. 3. Determination of Taxable Income. 4. Calculation of Income Tax Withholding. 5. Application of Post-Tax Deductions. 6. Finalization of Net Pay. The description must also explain where employer on-costs (e.g., Social Security, Payroll Tax) are calculated in parallel.
The Gross-to-Net calculation framework in Ghana is a structured process that requires a clear distinction between the earnings base for social security and the earnings base for income tax. This distinction is a fundamental architectural requirement for a compliant payroll system.
Step 1: Summation of Gross Earnings (Assessable Income)
The calculation begins by aggregating all forms of remuneration due to the employee for the pay period. This initial sum, known as Assessable Income, includes:
   * Basic Salary: The employee's fixed contractual salary.
   * Cash Allowances: All cash benefits, such as transport, housing, meal, and risk allowances.36
   * Variable Pay: Components like commissions, overtime payments, and the taxable portion of any bonus.
   * Benefits in Kind (BIK): The quantified monetary value of any non-cash benefits provided to the employee, such as the use of a company vehicle or accommodation. These are valued according to specific formulas set by the Ghana Revenue Authority (GRA).49

The sum of these components constitutes the employee's total gross earnings for tax purposes.
Step 2: Application of Pre-Tax Deductions
Next, statutory deductions that are permissible before the calculation of income tax are applied. In Ghana, this primarily consists of:
      * Mandatory Social Security Contributions (Employee Portion): The employee's 5.5% contribution to the Tier 1 SSNIT scheme is calculated based on their Basic Salary for the period, subject to the prevailing monthly minimum and maximum insurable earnings limits.10
      * Voluntary Pension Contributions (Tier 3): Any qualifying voluntary contributions made by the employee to a registered Tier 3 provident fund are also deductible, up to the statutory limit of 16.5% of basic salary (when combined with any employer contributions).13
Step 3: Determination of Taxable Income (Chargeable Income)
The Taxable Income, referred to as Chargeable Income in Ghanaian legislation, is derived by subtracting the total pre-tax deductions (from Step 2) from the Total Assessable Income (from Step 1).34


Chargeable Income=Assessable Income−(Mandatory SSNIT Contribution+Qualifying Voluntary Pension Contributions)
Step 4: Calculation of Income Tax Withholding (PAYE)
The PAYE tax is calculated on the Chargeable Income:
      * For resident individuals, the monthly progressive tax brackets are applied to the Chargeable Income on a cumulative basis to determine the tax liability for the period.10
      * For non-resident individuals, a flat tax rate of 25% is applied to the Chargeable Income.10
      * Any final withholding taxes on concessional payments (e.g., 5% tax on qualifying bonuses or overtime) are calculated separately on their respective bases and added to the main PAYE amount to determine the total tax to be withheld.36
Step 5: Application of Post-Tax Deductions
After the total tax liability is determined, it is subtracted from the income. Other post-tax deductions are then applied. These may include:
      * Repayments of employee loans.
      * Union dues.
      * Other voluntary contributions or deductions that do not qualify for tax relief.
Step 6: Finalization of Net Pay
The final amount remaining after all tax and post-tax deductions have been subtracted from the gross earnings is the employee's Net Pay. This is the final "take-home" amount to be paid to the employee.
Net Pay=Assessable Income−PreTax Deductions−Total Tax Withheld−PostTax Deductions
Parallel Calculation: Employer On-Costs
Calculated concurrently with the employee's G2N process are the mandatory statutory costs borne solely by the employer. These are not deducted from the employee's pay but represent an additional cost of employment to the company. In Ghana, this includes:
      * Employer's Mandatory Social Security Contribution: This is 13.0% of the employee's Basic Salary (subject to the same monthly floor and ceiling as the employee's contribution). This amount is further split, with 8.0% remitted to SSNIT for Tier 1 and 5.0% remitted to a private trustee for the Tier 2 scheme.50
      * Employer's Voluntary Pension Contributions: Any employer contributions to a Tier 3 provident fund.
These on-costs are essential for determining the total cost of employment and must be accrued and remitted alongside the employee's withheld amounts.
NOTE: For a new joiner, the cumulative payroll month count starts from the employee’s joining payroll month with the current employer. It does not start from January of the tax year. Example: if an employee joins on 15 March 2026 and is processed in March payroll, the cumulative month count in March is 1. April becomes 2, May becomes 3. Earlier months in the same tax year are not counted unless historical YTD payroll data for the same employment has been migrated into the system.

4.1 Minimum Wage


Is there a minimum wage? Who is eligible?
Yes, Ghana has a national minimum wage that is determined annually by the National Tripartite Committee (NTC). It is applicable to all workers, including those in full-time, part-time, and casual employment, with potential exemptions only for apprentices under specific training agreements.10
Is the minimum wage applicable at a national level or does it vary by province or state?
The minimum wage is set at a national level and applies uniformly across all regions and sectors of the country. There are no regional or provincial variations.56

Does the minimum wage impact any tax or social security calculations?
Yes, the minimum wage has a direct and significant impact on social security calculations. The monthly equivalent of the NDMW establishes the minimum insurable earnings floor for SSNIT contributions.59 Even if an employee's basic salary is below this floor, both employee and employer contributions must be calculated based on the statutory minimum insurable earnings.
How are deductions handled if they cannot be made due to minimum wage constraints? Are they carried forward or cancelled?
Statutory deductions, specifically PAYE tax and employee SSNIT contributions, are mandatory and must be withheld even if they result in the employee's net pay falling below the minimum wage threshold. The legal obligation to remit these amounts to the state authorities supersedes the take-home pay level. However, for non-statutory deductions like loan repayments or voluntary contributions, it is common business practice to defer or suspend these deductions if they would cause an employee's pay to fall below the statutory minimum. Such amounts are typically carried forward to be recovered in subsequent pay periods where sufficient net pay is available.


4.2 Salary Components


What are the mandatory and common salary components (e.g., basic salary, allowances, reimbursements)?
The salary structure in Ghana typically includes the following components:
      * Basic Salary: This is the fixed and guaranteed portion of an employee's remuneration, forming the basis for social security contributions.50
      * Allowances: These are common supplementary payments provided to employees. Frequent examples include Transport Allowance, Housing Allowance, and Meal Allowance.11
      * Reimbursements: These are payments made to compensate an employee for out-of-pocket expenses incurred for official business purposes.
      * Variable Pay: This includes performance-based payments like bonuses and commissions, as well as payments for extra hours worked (overtime).
Which components are taxable vs non-taxable and sociable vs non-sociable?
The distinction between taxable and sociable components is a critical aspect of Ghanaian payroll configuration. The system must be able to classify each pay element correctly to ensure accurate calculations.
      * Basic Salary: Is both taxable (part of total emoluments) and sociable (the base for SSNIT contributions).
      * Cash Allowances: Are taxable but non-sociable. They are included in the calculation of taxable income but are excluded from the base for SSNIT contributions.49
      * Overtime and Bonuses: Are taxable (often at special concessional rates) but are non-sociable.50
      * Reimbursements for Business Expenses: Are non-taxable and non-sociable, provided they are a direct reimbursement of actual, verifiable expenses incurred exclusively for the employer's business.49
Deliverable: Define the primary earnings base for social security contributions. Then, produce a comprehensive Inclusion/Exclusion analysis for this base, explaining for each payment type if it is included and why.
Primary Earnings Base Definition for Social Security
The primary and sole statutory earnings base for mandatory social security contributions (both Tier 1 and Tier 2) in Ghana is the employee's Basic Salary. 4.3 Proration


Are fixed wages prorated for the number of working days?
Yes, it is the standard and legally accepted business practice in Ghana to prorate fixed salary components for employees who do not work a full pay period, such as in the case of mid-period hires and terminations.64
Describe the statutory or standard business practice for prorating fixed salary components for mid-period hires and terminations.
While the Labour Act does not prescribe a specific mandatory formula, the most common and widely accepted method for proration is the working days method. This method is considered fair as it compensates the employee based on the actual number of days they were available for work within the pay period.

4.4 Overtime


Detail the statutory rules for overtime, including eligibility criteria and mandatory rates (e.g., 1.5x, 2.0x).
Under the Labour Act, 2003 (Act 651), the standard work week is a maximum of 40 hours, typically spread over five 8-hour days.65 Any work performed beyond these standard hours is considered overtime. The law mandates that overtime work must be compensated at a premium rate of at least
1.5 times (150%) the employee's normal hourly rate of pay.14 While higher rates can be set by collective agreement or employment contract (e.g., 200% for weekends or public holidays), 150% is the statutory minimum.
Explain how overtime is treated for income tax and social security calculations.
            * Social Security Treatment: Overtime pay is non-sociable. It is not included in the basic salary base for calculating SSNIT contributions.
            * Income Tax Treatment: The tax treatment of overtime depends on the employee's status. A significant edge case exists for "junior staff," which requires specific system logic.
            * For Junior Staff: An employee is classified as a junior staff for this purpose if their total annual qualifying employment income does not exceed GHS 18,000.10 For these employees, a concessional final withholding tax applies:
            * If the overtime payment in a month is not more than 50% of their monthly basic salary, the entire overtime amount is taxed at 5%.
            * If the overtime payment exceeds 50% of their monthly basic salary, the portion above the 50% threshold is taxed at 10%.10
            * For All Other Employees: For employees who do not meet the "junior staff" criteria, all overtime earnings are added to their total assessable income and are taxed at their marginal progressive tax rate.


4.5 Bonuses


Is there a statutory requirement to pay bonuses? If so, how are they calculated?
No, there is no statutory requirement in Ghana for an employer to pay a bonus or a "13th-month salary".12 The payment of bonuses is discretionary or as stipulated in an employment contract or collective bargaining agreement.
Is there a specific tax treatment for bonuses?
Yes, Ghana has a specific concessional tax treatment for bonus payments, which is a critical element for payroll configuration.
            * Concessional Rule: The portion of an employee's total annual bonus that is up to 15% of their annual basic salary is subject to a final withholding tax of 5%.10
            * Treatment of Excess: Any bonus amount paid in excess of this 15% threshold is not eligible for the concessional rate. Instead, the excess amount must be added to the employee's regular assessable income for the period and taxed at the standard progressive income tax rates.36
This rule necessitates that the payroll system maintains a year-to-date accumulator for both basic salary and bonuses paid to accurately determine when the 15% threshold is crossed and apply the correct tax treatment.


4.6 Wage Classification (Country-Specific)


Are there country-specific classifications for wages (e.g., Ordinary vs. Additional Wages) that are critical for social security or tax calculations?
Not Applicable. Ghana's payroll legislation does not use a formal wage classification system like "Ordinary Wage" and "Additional Wage." Instead, the necessary distinction for statutory calculations is achieved by differentiating between 'Basic Salary' and 'Total Emoluments' (or Assessable Income). 'Basic Salary' serves as the base for social security, while 'Total Emoluments' (which includes basic salary, all cash allowances, and taxable benefits) serves as the starting point for income tax calculations. This fundamental distinction must be embedded in the architecture of the payroll system's earnings elements.


Module 5: Social Security & Pension Systems




Purpose: To detail the rules for all mandatory social security and pension schemes.


This module outlines the structure, contribution rates, and calculation logic for Ghana's mandatory pension system. These contributions represent the primary pre-tax deduction in the Gross-to-Net sequence and require precise configuration of both the contribution base and the remittance process.


5.1 Contribution Types and Rates


List all mandatory social security and pension contributions for both employer and employee.
Ghana operates a mandatory Three-Tier Pension Scheme under the National Pensions Act, 2008 (Act 766). The first two tiers are mandatory for all employees in the formal sector, while the third is voluntary.54
            * Tier 1: Basic National Social Security Scheme: This is a defined benefit scheme managed by the state-run Social Security and National Insurance Trust (SSNIT). It provides monthly pensions for old age, invalidity, and survivor's benefits.54 A portion of this contribution (2.5%) is allocated to the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) to cover health insurance premiums for active contributors.61
            * Tier 2: Occupational Pension Scheme: This is a mandatory, privately managed, defined contribution scheme. Employers must select a private pension trustee, licensed by the National Pensions Regulatory Authority (NPRA), to manage these funds. This tier provides a lump-sum payment to employees upon retirement.54
            * Tier 3: Voluntary Provident Fund: This is an optional, privately managed scheme that allows both employees and employers to make additional contributions to enhance retirement savings.54
Are contributions managed at a national or sub-national level?
All mandatory and voluntary pension contributions are regulated and managed at the national level.


5.1.1 Contribution Basis Definition


For each mandatory contribution (e.g., social security, pensions), provide the statutory definition of the earnings base (e.g., 'Gross Remuneration,' 'Sociable Income,' 'Contributory Salary'). Clarify the legal definitions of any specific terms used and explain how this base is derived from an employee's total gross pay.
For all mandatory contributions under Tier 1 and Tier 2 of the pension scheme, the statutory definition of the earnings base is the employee's Basic Salary. This term is consistently interpreted and applied to mean the fixed, regular, and contractual salary of an employee, explicitly excluding any variable or supplementary payments such as cash allowances (e.g., transport, housing), overtime, bonuses, and commissions.50
Therefore, to derive the contribution base, the payroll system must isolate the 'Basic Salary' component from the employee's total gross pay. All other earnings elements are disregarded for the purpose of calculating social security contributions.

5.2 Calculation Logic and Edge Cases


What is the step-by-step calculation logic for all types of social security contributions?
            1. Identify the Base: Determine the employee’s Basic Salary for the pay period. Only Basic Salary is pensionable for mandatory Tier 1 and Tier 2 contributions; allowances, overtime, bonuses, commissions, and other variable payments are excluded..
            2. Apply Floor and Ceiling:
            * If the Basic Salary is less than the monthly minimum insurable earning (GHS 478.94 for Tier 2 and Tier 1 - 587.80), use the minimum earning as the calculation base. 
            * If the Basic Salary is greater than the monthly maximum insurable earning (GHS 69,000), use the maximum earning as the calculation base.
            * Otherwise, use the actual Basic Salary.
            3. Calculate Employee Contribution: Multiply the adjusted base from Step 2 by 5.5%. This amount is a pre-tax deduction.
            4. Calculate Employer Contribution: Multiply the adjusted base from Step 2 by 13.0%. This is an employer on-cost.
            5. Determine Remittance Amounts:
            * The amount to be remitted to SSNIT (Tier 1) is the base salary (as adjusted in Step 2) multiplied by 13.5%.
            * The amount to be remitted to the private trustee (Tier 2) is the base salary (as adjusted in Step 2) multiplied by 5.0%.
Note: The base salary for computation of your pension contributions under the 2-Tier pension scheme for 2025 and 2026 should be GHS 439.34 and GHS 478.94 respectively by Tier 2 trustee operational requirement for 2026.
            * Please be informed that GRA  will NOT accept any contribution which is calculated on a basic salary below that of the base pay stated above effective immediately.
Describe the specific rules covering eligibility, joining month, termination month, mid-period hires, mid-period terminations, and mid-period salary changes.
            * Eligibility: All employees in the formal sector are required to be enrolled in the mandatory Tier 1 and Tier 2 schemes from their first day of employment. This includes employees on probation, temporary contracts, or part-time arrangements.61 New entrants to the scheme must be between the ages of 15 and 45.74
            * Mid-Period Hires/Terminations: For employees who join or leave mid-month, contributions are calculated based on the prorated basic salary earned during their period of employment within that month. The monthly floor and ceiling are generally not prorated for such periods.
            * Mid-Period Salary Changes: If an employee's basic salary changes mid-pay period, the contribution should be calculated based on the total basic salary earned in that period (i.e., the sum of the prorated salary at the old rate and the prorated salary at the new rate).
Are there any variations in rates or eligibility based on employee age or gender?
No, the contribution rates and general eligibility for the mandatory schemes do not vary based on an employee's age or gender.
How are contributions calculated for the Board of Directors who are also employees?
If a member of the Board of Directors also has an employment contract with the company and receives a regular salary for their executive role, they are considered an employee for payroll purposes. Mandatory SSNIT contributions must be calculated and remitted based on the basic salary portion of their remuneration, subject to the same rules as any other employee.14 Fees paid purely for their role as a director (e.g., for attending board meetings) are typically not considered part of basic salary and are therefore not sociable.


5.3 Voluntary Contributions & Private Schemes


Can employees and employers make additional voluntary contributions?
Yes. The Three-Tier Pension Scheme includes a Tier 3 Voluntary Provident Fund and Personal Pension Scheme. Both employees and employers can make additional contributions to these privately managed schemes to supplement the mandatory Tier 1 and Tier 2 benefits.54
Which employee contributions are allowed as pre-tax deductions?
Employee contributions to a registered Tier 3 voluntary scheme are allowed as a pre-tax deduction. This tax relief is subject to a combined limit for both employee and employer contributions of 16.5% of the employee's basic salary.13 Any amount contributed above this 16.5% cap is not tax-deductible and must be treated as a post-tax deduction.
What documents or declarations are required from an employee for these contributions?
To initiate voluntary Tier 3 contributions via payroll deduction, an employee must typically complete an enrollment form for the chosen private pension provider and submit a written authorization to the employer. This declaration should specify the amount or percentage of their basic salary to be deducted and contributed to the scheme.


Module 6: Income Tax Withholding System




Purpose: To define the entire income tax withholding system.


This module details the methodology, rates, and logic of Ghana's personal income tax system. A correct configuration of this module is essential for ensuring compliance with the Ghana Revenue Authority (GRA) and for accurately calculating employee take-home pay.


6.1 System Overview and Methodology


Describe the country's income tax withholding system (e.g., PAYE, PAYG).
Ghana operates a Pay-As-You-Earn (PAYE) system for employment income.10 Under this system, the employer is legally obligated to calculate and withhold income tax from the employee's emoluments each pay period and remit the withheld amount to the Ghana Revenue Authority (GRA) on the employee's behalf.53
What types of taxes are withheld by the employer, including any temporary or upcoming government levies?
Employers are primarily responsible for withholding:
            1. Personal Income Tax (PAYE): Calculated using a progressive rate schedule for residents and a flat rate for non-residents.
            2. Final Withholding Taxes: On specific payments like qualifying bonuses and overtime for junior staff.
            3. Social Security Contributions (Employee's Share): While not a tax, this 5.5% is a mandatory withholding remitted to SSNIT.
As of FY 2025, there are no other temporary levies (like the previous COVID-19 Health Recovery Levy on goods and services) that are directly withheld from employee salaries via payroll.
At what level are tax rates published (central or state)?
All personal income tax rates and brackets are set and published at the central (national) level by the Government of Ghana and administered by the Ghana Revenue Authority.


6.1.1 Taxable Income Definition


Provide the statutory definition of 'Taxable Income' (or equivalent legislative terms like 'Gross Taxable Income,' 'Assessable Income,' 'Gross Remuneration'). Explain how this base is derived from total gross earnings, clarifying the legal definitions of all key terms and the sequence of adjustments (e.g., exemptions, pre-tax deductions) applied to arrive at the final taxable amount.
In Ghanaian tax legislation, the key terms leading to the final taxable amount are 'Assessable Income' and 'Chargeable Income'.
            1. Assessable Income: This is the starting point and represents the employee's total gross earnings from employment for the period.52 It is a broad measure that includes all gains and profits from employment, whether paid in cash or provided in kind. Key inclusions are:
            * Basic Salary and Wages
            * All Cash Allowances (e.g., housing, transport, entertainment)
            * Bonuses (the portion exceeding the 15% concessional limit), Commissions, and Overtime
            * The quantified monetary value of Benefits in Kind (BIK), such as accommodation and vehicle usage, valued per statutory formulas.49
            2. Chargeable Income (Taxable Income): This is the final amount upon which the PAYE tax rates are applied. It is derived by subtracting allowable pre-tax deductions from the Assessable Income.34 The sequence of adjustment is as follows:

Chargeable Income=Assessable Income−Allowable Deductions
The primary allowable deductions processed through payroll are:
               * The employee's mandatory 5.5% SSNIT contribution.
               * Qualifying employee contributions to a voluntary Tier 3 pension scheme (up to the 16.5% combined limit).
Therefore, the taxable income base is significantly broader than the social security base, as it includes all allowances and benefits in kind.

Decision Guide: Determining the Correct Tax Schedule

               * Question: Has the employee been present in Ghana for an aggregate period of 183 days or more in any 12-month period that begins or ends in the current tax year?

               * No: The employee is a Non-Resident. Apply the Non-Resident Tax Schedule.

The official tax calculation method in Ghana is a cumulative annual system, administered through monthly withholdings. While employers calculate and withhold PAYE on a monthly basis, the ultimate tax liability is determined by the total income earned over the entire calendar year.4
The monthly PAYE calculation uses a prorated version of the annual tax brackets. The calculation is applied on a year-to-date (YTD) basis to ensure that tax withholding is smoothed over the year and accurately reflects the employee's projected annual earnings.
Step-by-Step Monthly Cumulative Calculation Method:
               1. Calculate Current Month's Chargeable Income: As determined in Section 6.1.1.
               2. Determine Cumulative YTD Chargeable Income: Add the current month's chargeable income to the YTD chargeable income from the previous month.
               3. Calculate Cumulative YTD Tax: Apply the annual graduated tax brackets to the cumulative YTD chargeable income.
               * Formula: YTD Tax = Tax on (YTD Chargeable Income) using the annual tax table.
               4. Determine Current Month's Tax: Subtract the total tax withheld in previous months of the year from the new cumulative YTD tax calculated in Step 3.
               * Formula: Current Month's Tax = Cumulative YTD Tax - YTD Tax Withheld Previously

How does tax applicability and calculation logic differ by residential status?
The difference is fundamental:

6.3 Adjustments and Special Cases


Are year-end tax adjustments required?
Yes. The employer is required to perform a final tax reconciliation at the end of the year and submit an Employer's Annual Tax Deduction Schedule to the GRA by 31 March of the following year.2 This report summarizes the total emoluments paid and total tax withheld for each employee for the entire calendar year. Any discrepancies between tax withheld and the final calculated annual tax liability should be corrected.
Furthermore, all individual employees are now required to file their own annual personal income tax return by 30 April, where they must declare all sources of income and reconcile their total tax liability.40
Are there minimum or higher tax requirements if employees fail to submit a tax ID or social security number?
Since the Ghana Card PIN is now the mandatory TIN and SSNIT identifier, an employee cannot be compliantly onboarded to payroll without it.37 Failure to provide the number means the employer cannot correctly register the employee with the authorities or remit contributions and taxes on their behalf, leading to non-compliance for both parties. There is no provision for a "penalty tax rate" in lieu of a TIN; rather, the process cannot proceed compliantly without it.
What is the income tax and social security refund procedure? Is it processed by the employer or the government?
Any overpayment of income tax discovered at year-end is processed and refunded by the Ghana Revenue Authority (GRA), not the employer. The employee must apply to the Commissioner-General for a refund of the excess tax paid.83
For social security, overpayments are typically handled as a credit against future contributions by SSNIT.

Are non-cash benefits taxed via the standard payroll system, or a separate employer-paid regime (e.g., Fringe Benefits Tax)?

What is the definition of fringe benefits, its pay cycle, and how is the taxable amount evaluated?

The primary BIKs with defined valuation rules are for accommodation and vehicle usage. The value is calculated as a percentage of the employee's total cash emoluments for the period.

Is there a statutory priority order if an employee has multiple garnishments but insufficient net pay to cover them all?

Module 8: Statutory Leave Management




Purpose: To define the rules for all statutory leave types.


This module details the statutory requirements for leave entitlement, accrual, and payment. Accurate leave management is crucial as it directly impacts salary calculations (especially for unpaid leave), termination payouts, and overall compliance with the Labour Act, 2003.


8.1 Leave Entitlements


For each type of statutory leave (e.g., annual, sick, parental), what are the eligibility criteria and accrual methodology?
               * Annual Leave:
               * Eligibility: An employee is entitled to annual leave after completing 12 months of continuous service with an employer.85
               * Accrual/Entitlement: The statutory minimum entitlement is 15 working days of fully paid leave for each year of service.85 The leave is granted and does not accrue on a monthly basis in the first year; it becomes an entitlement after 12 months of service.
               * Sick Leave:
               * Eligibility: An employee is entitled to sick leave if their absence is certified by a recognized medical practitioner.85
               * Accrual/Entitlement: The Labour Act does not specify a maximum number of paid sick leave days per year.67 The entitlement is based on the duration certified by the medical practitioner. While the law does not explicitly state that sick leave must be paid, the common practice and legal presumption is that it is paid unless the employment contract states otherwise.87
               * Maternity Leave:
               * Eligibility: A female employee is eligible upon presenting a medical certificate confirming her pregnancy and expected date of confinement.89
               * Accrual/Entitlement: The statutory minimum entitlement is 12 weeks of fully paid maternity leave.67 Recent proposals aim to increase this to 14 weeks, but as of FY 2025, the 12-week rule under the current Labour Act applies.90 This leave can be extended for medical reasons related to the pregnancy or confinement.
               * Paternity Leave:
               * Eligibility & Entitlement: The current Labour Act, 2003 does not provide for statutory paternity leave. However, a new Labour Bill is under consideration which proposes an entitlement of at least five days.90 Until this bill is passed into law, any paternity leave is based on company policy or collective agreement.
How are unpaid or partially paid leaves handled? Explain how this impacts the calculation of prorated salary and the accrual of other entitlements, illustrating with a sample calculation.
When an employee takes unpaid leave, their fixed salary components for that pay period must be prorated. The proration is typically done using the working days method. This absence reduces the gross salary, which in turn reduces the calculated SSNIT contributions and PAYE tax for the period. Accrual of entitlements like annual leave is generally based on continuous service, and a short period of approved unpaid leave does not typically break this continuity.
               * Sample Calculation (Unpaid Leave):
               * Scenario: An employee with a monthly basic salary of GHS 3,000 takes 5 days of unpaid leave in a month with 20 working days.
               * Calculation:
               1. Daily Rate = GHS 3,000 / 20 working days = GHS 150 per day.
               2. Deduction for Unpaid Leave = 5 days * GHS 150/day = GHS 750.
               3. Prorated Basic Salary for the month = GHS 3,000 - GHS 750 = GHS 2,250.
               * Impact: The employee's SSNIT contributions and PAYE tax for that month will be calculated based on the reduced basic salary of GHS 2,250.
How are unused paid leaves treated upon termination? Provide an example computation of leave encashment, specifying which salary components are considered.
Upon termination of employment, an employee is entitled to be paid for any accrued but unused annual leave.85 The payment, or leave encashment, should be calculated based on the employee's full remuneration.
               * Example Computation (Leave Encashment):
               * Scenario: An employee terminates employment with 10 days of accrued, unused annual leave. Their monthly basic salary is GHS 2,400, and they receive a fixed monthly cash allowance of GHS 600. The month has 20 working days.
               * Salary Components Considered: Both basic salary and fixed allowances are typically included to calculate the daily rate for encashment.
               * Calculation:
               1. Total Monthly Remuneration = GHS 2,400 (Basic) + GHS 600 (Allowance) = GHS 3,000.
               2. Daily Rate = GHS 3,000 / 20 working days = GHS 150 per day.
               3. Leave Encashment Payout = 10 days * GHS 150/day = GHS 1,500.
               * Tax Treatment: This payout is considered part of the employee's final emoluments and is subject to PAYE tax. It is generally not subject to SSNIT contributions.

9.1 Termination Payments


What are the different types of termination payments? How are they calculated?
Upon termination, an employee may be entitled to several types of payments:
               * Final Salary: Prorated salary up to the last day of employment.
               * Payment in Lieu of Notice (PILON): If the employer terminates the contract without providing the statutory notice period, they must pay the employee the salary they would have earned during that period.11 The notice period is typically 2 weeks for contracts under 3 years and 1 month for contracts of 3 years or more.11
               * Accrued Leave Encashment: Payment for any outstanding annual leave balance, as detailed in Module 8.
               * Redundancy/Severance Pay: Applicable only in cases of termination due to redundancy.
Detail the calculation and tax withholding rules for unused leave paid out on termination, specifying any special codes or rates.
Payment for unused leave upon termination is calculated based on the employee's daily rate of pay (including basic salary and fixed allowances) multiplied by the number of accrued leave days. This amount is added to the employee's final assessable income and is subject to standard PAYE tax at their marginal rate. There are no special tax codes or concessional rates for leave encashment.34
Describe the rules for statutory severance/redundancy payments that qualify for concessional tax treatment.
Termination due to redundancy occurs when an employer's business needs change, leading to the elimination of a role.94
               * Calculation: The Labour Act does not prescribe a specific formula for calculating redundancy pay. The amount and terms are subject to negotiation between the employer and the employee (or their trade union).92 Common practice often bases the calculation on the employee's length of service (e.g., one month's pay for each year of service).95
               * Tax Treatment: Redundancy pay is exempt from income tax.34 This is a significant tax concession. The full negotiated amount is paid to the employee without any PAYE withholding.
Deliverable: For other Employment Termination Payments (ETPs), provide a reference guide describing each ETP code and when it applies.
Not Applicable. Ghana's tax system does not use a system of specific codes for different types of Employment Termination Payments in the way that some other jurisdictions (like Australia) do. Payments are generally classified by their nature (e.g., salary, leave pay, redundancy pay) to determine the correct tax treatment.


9.2 Irregular and Retrospective Payments


Describe the specific tax calculation method required for irregular payments like bonuses, commissions, and back pay.
               * Bonuses: As detailed in Module 4, bonuses have a two-tier tax treatment. The portion up to 15% of the annual basic salary is taxed at a final rate of 5%. The excess is added to regular income and taxed at progressive rates.36
               * Commissions and Back Pay: These payments are treated as regular employment income. They are added to the employee's assessable income for the period in which they are paid. The tax is calculated using the standard cumulative PAYE method, which will account for the increased income and apply the correct marginal tax rates.83
For arrears, how are contributions and deductions computed? Should calculations use the rates and limits from the original earning period or the period of payment?
Statutory contributions and tax deductions for arrears payments (back pay) should be calculated using the rates, thresholds, and regulations in effect during the period of payment. The PAYE and SSNIT systems operate on a "cash basis," meaning income is taxed and contributions are assessed when the payment is actually made, not when it was earned.4 Therefore, the payroll system should apply the current month's tax brackets and SSNIT ceilings to the total payment.

The mandatory year-end finalization process involves reconciling all payroll data for the calendar year (1 January to 31 December) and submitting a final summary report to the GRA.

11.2 Special Employee Groups & Income


How does taxation and social security differ for part-time employees and the Board of Directors?
               * Part-Time Employees:
               * Social Security: Part-time employees are subject to the same mandatory SSNIT contribution rules as full-time employees. Contributions (5.5% employee, 13.0% employer) must be made based on their basic salary, subject to the monthly minimum and maximum insurable earnings limits.61
               * Taxation: Their income is subject to PAYE tax. The standard progressive tax rates are applied to their earnings. The cumulative calculation method naturally adjusts for their lower income levels.
               * Board of Directors:
               * Directors with Employment Contracts: If a director also serves in an executive capacity and receives a regular salary, they are treated as an employee. Their salary is subject to both PAYE and SSNIT contributions.14
               * Directors Receiving Fees Only: If a director only receives fees for their board duties (e.g., attending meetings), these fees are considered investment income, not employment income. They are typically subject to a final withholding tax and are not subject to SSNIT contributions.
How is taxation and social security handled for employees with multiple employers?
               * Social Security: An employee with multiple employers must have SSNIT contributions paid by each employer. Each employer calculates and remits contributions based on the basic salary they pay, subject to the monthly floor and ceiling.61 The contributions from all employments are consolidated under the employee's single SSNIT account.
               * Taxation: Each employer withholds PAYE independently, based on the income they pay, using the standard monthly tax brackets. This can result in an under-withholding of total tax for the year, as neither employer is aware of the employee's total aggregated income. The employee is legally responsible for consolidating all their income sources on their annual personal income tax return and paying any additional tax due to the GRA by the 30 April deadline.83
What is the treatment of foreign-sourced employment income for residents?
Resident individuals in Ghana are taxed on their worldwide income.34 Therefore, foreign-sourced employment income is generally taxable in Ghana. However, two important provisions apply:
               1. Exemption: An exemption exists for employment income earned by a resident who works outside Ghana for more than 183 days in a 12-month period for a non-resident employer.34
               2. Foreign Tax Credit: Where the foreign income is taxable in Ghana and tax has also been paid in the foreign country, the individual can claim a foreign tax credit to avoid double taxation. The credit is typically limited to the amount of Ghanaian tax that would be payable on that foreign income.97
How do employer stock option plans work and how are they taxed?
The Ghanaian tax laws do not contain specific, detailed provisions on the taxation of employee stock option plans (ESOPs). In the absence of specific rules, the general principles of taxing employment income would apply.98 The benefit derived by the employee would likely be considered a benefit in kind.
Based on international best practice, which the GRA would likely follow, the taxable event would be the exercise of the option. The taxable income would be the "spread" – the difference between the Fair Market Value (FMV) of the stock at the date of exercise and the price the employee paid to exercise the option (the strike price). This amount would be added to the employee's assessable income and taxed at their marginal progressive rate. Any subsequent gain upon the sale of the shares would be a capital gain.

Module 13: Common Business Practices & Non-Statutory Rules




Purpose: To capture prevalent, non-statutory conventions that are essential for a functional and user-friendly payroll system.


This module addresses common operational practices that, while not explicitly mandated by law, are essential for the smooth functioning of payroll in a Ghanaian context. Incorporating these practices into the system design enhances user experience and aligns with local business expectations.
Describe the common practice for off-cycle or ad-hoc payments (e.g., paying a missed bonus). Are they typically processed as standalone payments with their own payslip, or are they added to the next regular payroll cycle?
The most common and administratively simpler practice for handling off-cycle or ad-hoc payments is to include them in the next regular payroll cycle.77 This approach ensures that the additional income is correctly subjected to the cumulative tax calculation for the month and that all statutory deductions are handled properly within a single process.
Processing a standalone, off-cycle payment is less common due to the complexity of accurately calculating cumulative tax and ensuring timely remittance for a single payment. If an urgent off-cycle payment is made, it is typically a net payment, with the gross-up and statutory calculations processed retrospectively in the next regular payroll run to ensure full compliance.
How are non-recoverable draws against future commissions or sales incentives typically handled for tax and social security purposes?
A non-recoverable draw is effectively a guaranteed salary payment. As such, it is treated as regular employment income in the period it is paid. It is included in the employee's assessable income and is subject to PAYE tax. For social security, if the draw is structured as an advance on the basic salary component, it would be subject to SSNIT contributions. If it is purely an advance on variable commissions, it would be treated as non-sociable.
Are there common non-statutory leave types (e.g., 'duvet days,' extra birthday leave, volunteer days)? How are they typically managed and reported?
While not widespread, some companies, particularly multinational corporations, may offer non-statutory leave types such as birthday leave, study leave, or volunteer days. These are governed entirely by company policy.
                  * Management: These leave types are managed through the company's internal HR and time management systems.
                  * Reporting: As they are typically paid leave, they do not require special reporting. The employee's salary is paid as normal, and the leave is simply tracked against their non-statutory entitlement. There is no impact on statutory payroll calculations unless the leave is taken as unpaid.
